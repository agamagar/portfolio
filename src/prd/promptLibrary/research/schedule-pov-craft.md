# Schedule POV, the craft: research dossier
_Researched 2026-09-15. Sources listed at the bottom, numbered; cite by number inline._

Scope: the vocabulary and conventions of the first-person / point-of-view (POV) frame, so that prompts for the Schedule feature (a person in an Indian city seen from their own eyes, their own hands entering from the bottom edge holding a phone or doing a task) use the words the craft uses. The five reference frames we are matching: (a) looking down at a breakfast table, own hands on a mug beside a tablet and toast; (b) lying on one's back in bed, phone held up above the face, wall and posters beyond; (c) looking straight down at a phone in one's own hand, the room floor far below; (d) walking while pulling a suitcase, own arm extended, motion blur on the floor; (e) being pulled forward by someone's hand, ultra-wide, motion-blurred field.

Tiers: TEXTBOOK / GLOSSARY = Bordwell and Thompson via a university glossary, film-school and dictionary entries; PRACTITIONER = a named cinematographer, photographer, VFX supervisor or stock editor describing a method; MANUFACTURER = Apple, GoPro, Meta, Axon documentation; INDEX = stock-library keyword pages (evidence of what the commercial world calls the shot). Everything not marked "(inference)" or UNVERIFIED was read on the cited page. Where a page was reached only through a text-extraction proxy (r.jina.ai) that is noted in the source list; the content is the page's own.

Headline: in cinema the POV shot is certified by two things a still photograph does not have, the cut (a shot of the character looking, before or after) and the movement (handheld, breathing, staring). A still has to carry the certification inside a single frame, and every landmark POV film and every stock-photo taxonomy agrees on how: the viewer's own body, hands first, entering from the bottom edge, seen from eye height, through a wide lens. The single most useful sentence found is from the Hardcore Henry rig designer: "The more evident the human body in the frame is, the more immersion is happening on the screen" [14].

## What the medium actually is

### The definition, and its two halves
- TEXTBOOK. Bordwell and Thompson, Film Art (10th ed.), as reproduced by the University of West Georgia glossary: a point-of-view shot is "A shot taken with the camera placed approximately where the character's eyes would be, showing what the character would see" [1]. The MIT film lexicon adds the editing half: "A shot implying a character's field of vision, showing what the character sees; usually cut in before and/or after a shot of the character looking" [2]. The eyeline match is the cut that certifies it: "the first shot shows a person looking off in one direction and the second shows a nearby space containing what he or she sees" [1]; "The alternation of a shot showing a person looking offscreen with a shot of what that person is looking at" [2].
- GLOSSARY. Columbia's Film Language Glossary: "With POV, the audience is, in effect, looking through the character's eye" [3]. Illinois (Rushing): "A POV shot shows us the world from one particular character's perspective, as if we were seeing through his or her eyes", noting POV shots often use handheld camera work and questioning whether the device really produces identification, especially with antagonists [4]. A Dictionary of Media and Communication (Oxford Reference) defines subjective camerawork as "the use of the camera to show us events as if from a particular participant's visual point of view (encouraging viewers to identify with that person's way of seeing events)", "typically hand-held", and as something that "represents exclusively what a particular person is seeing" [5].
- Synonyms. Wikipedia lists the shot as "point-of-view shot (also called POV shot, first-person shot, or subjective camera)" and says the device relies on "shot sequencing (e.g. shot/reverse shot), sound effects, visual effects and acting" to be read [6]. HowToFilmSchool gives the three-beat structure: "first, the character looks then, the film shows what they see sometimes, the film cuts back to the character reacting", and stresses that POV is about "narrative perspective, not scientific accuracy" [7]. Filmmakers Academy: the subjective camera "involves the viewers directly in the action", uses "panning or tilting to simulate the eye of a directly involved person", and the POV shot is its "most extreme form" [8].
- PRACTITIONER. Blain Brown, Cinematography: Theory and Practice: "It is a standard operating procedure in film that true POV shots are almost always done handheld"; the camera "moves with a human motion: not too steady and always following the action as if staring"; and a fully subjective POV would have other characters look straight into the lens, which classical narrative avoids because it makes the audience "aware that they are watching the movie" [9]. StudioBinder's how-to: "Position the camera at the character's eye level to exactly match their line of sight. Use a handheld or Steadicam setup to mimic natural head movements and complete the illusion" [10]; "Often shot with a hand-held camera to accentuate the subjective perspective" [11]. No Film School: choose "a wide-angle lens that resembles human vision", keep the movement steady "with natural bumpiness", and let "objects (branches, curtains) pass in front of the lens for authenticity" [12].

So the cinema definition has two halves: WHERE the camera is (the character's eyes, at their eye level) and HOW the viewer is told so (the glance shot before it, and the human wobble). A still has only the first half and must fake the second with in-frame evidence (inference from [1][2][9][10]).

### Landmark uses and what each one teaches about the single frame
- Napoléon (1927). In the snowball-fight sequence the camera "defends itself as if it were Bonaparte himself... as if it were human. A punch in the lens. Arms at the side of the camera as if the camera itself had arms" [6]. Lesson: arms beside the lens are the oldest first-person cue there is.
- Lady in the Lake (1947). "The camera represents the point of view of the protagonist, while other characters talk directly to the camera"; Marlowe's "face is shown only in reflections"; MGM made Montgomery add a prologue because he appeared so rarely. Technically, cinematographer John Arnold built "a modified camera dolly with four independent wheels, allowing it to walk through doors and up stairs", and fights used "a modified Eyemo camera with a flexible shoulder harness". The New York Times' Thomas Pryor grew tired of "seeing a hand reaching toward a door knob, or lighting a cigarette or lifting a glass, or a door moving toward you"; Bosley Crowther argued "the camera character should not talk at all because that destroys the illusion of complete participation by the audience" [13]. Lesson: the catalogue of first-person stills is literally Pryor's complaint: a hand doing a task at the bottom of the frame, an object coming toward the lens, a face only in a mirror.
- Halloween (1978). Shot largely on "a Panaglide, a clone of the Steadicam"; the opening murder is "seen through the eye holes of Michael's clown costume mask"; historian Nicholas Rogers on the "unmounted first-person camera" that invited viewers "to adopt the murderer's assaultive gaze and to hear his heavy breathing and plodding footsteps" [19]. The Calgary festival's production history: "only the second production to ever film with" the Steadicam, "the mammoth, four-minute, one-take POV shot" burned through "three camera operators" over "16 hours" [20]. Lesson: an occluder at the frame edge (mask eye-holes) and breathing are movement-era cues; for a still, the edge occluder survives, the breathing has to become blur.
- Peep Show (2003 to 2015). Cameras were "strapped to the actors' heads, or attached to a hat"; otherwise "scenes are filmed with the camera being held over the actor's shoulder, or directly in front of their face"; "The quality of footage captured with this method is sometimes poor and the technique was used less and less in later series" [17]. The British Comedy Guide: "Much of the filming in the first series and part of the second series was carried out with the actors wearing head cameras"; later "the actors talk directly into a normal camera (as, according to Webb, this produces the same effect but is easier to do, and the picture quality is better)" [18]. Lesson: the head-mount is not what makes the shot; eye-contact into the lens and the eye-height position do. That is also the Blain Brown warning turned into a house style [9].
- Enter the Void (2009). Noé took the idea from Lady in the Lake; the film is "shot from a first-person viewpoint with extensive uses of long take", and "Most of the film takes place in Oscar's first-person perspective"; in flashbacks Noé shows "Oscar's head and shoulders within the frame... rather than letting the camera be the character's eyes" because that is how he sees himself in dreams [16]. The blink blackouts the film is famous for are described in search-result snippets (TV Tropes, Arrow Films) and in Jeeshan Gazi's Film Criticism essay, but the essay sat behind a CAPTCHA and the fetched Wikipedia text does not mention blinks: UNVERIFIED here. Lesson: over-the-head-with-shoulders is a different register from eyes-camera; if the head is in frame it is no longer a POV still.
- Hardcore Henry (2015). Shot on two GoPro Hero 3s in a custom "Adventure Mask" after "more than 30 rig prototypes were destroyed" [15]. Rig designer Sergey Valyaev on why the standard head mount failed: "if you attach a GoPro to your forehead and bend forward, the camera will capture only half of your body"; the mask placed the camera at the mouth: "Having the camera at this position allows the viewer to see the main body from the shoulders and chest. The more evident the human body in the frame is, the more immersion is happening on the screen." A magnetic stabiliser "smoothed out small camera fluctuations while walking" without removing the shake, and the VFX team called the GoPro footage "a pretty extreme lens distortion" [14]. Lesson: this is the rule for our stills. Lower the camera to chin height and tilt down, and the chest and shoulders edge into the bottom of the frame; the body is the proof.
- The anti-term. A SnorriCam "(also chestcam, body mount/bodymount, or bodycam) is a camera device used in filmmaking that is rigged to the body of the actor, with the camera facing the actor directly so that they appear in a fixed position in the center of the frame"; "The type of shot that this device is used for can be considered opposite to the more common fixed first-person perspective shot" [21]. "Body-mounted" in a prompt can therefore summon the wrong shot; say what the camera looks AT, not what it is bolted to (inference from [21]).
- Theory. Edward Branigan's Point of View in the Cinema (Mouton, 1984) catalogues eight POV structures (closed, delayed, open, continuing, cheated, multiple, embedded, reciprocal) [22]; his six-element breakdown (origin, vision, time, frame, object, mind) appeared only in a search snippet and is UNVERIFIED here. Mascelli's The Five C's of Cinematography is cited as a source on Wikipedia's POV page [6] but the full text was not accessible; Arijon's Grammar of the Film Language: nothing solid found online for his treatment of the subjective camera. Neither is cited for any claim below.

### The conventions that make a frame read as "my eyes" (consolidated)
1. Camera at the character's eye position and eye level [1][10]; on the body, that means eye height for a level view, and the Hardcore Henry finding that dropping to mouth or chin height brings the shoulders and chest in when looking down [14].
2. Own body evidence entering from the bottom of the frame: arms "at the side of the camera" [6], "a hand reaching toward a door knob... lifting a glass" [13], "the main body from the shoulders and chest" [14], "your arms and legs" from a chest mount [44].
3. No head, no face, except in a reflection [13]; if head and shoulders are in shot it is the over-the-head register, not the eyes [16].
4. Other people look into the lens as if at you [9][13][18].
5. Human unsteadiness: handheld, "not too steady", "natural bumpiness" [9][12], breathing [19]; for a still, motion blur with the hands sharp [23].
6. Things crossing or approaching the lens: "a door moving toward you" [13], "branches, curtains" [12], mask eye-holes [19].
7. A wide lens "that resembles human vision" [12], accepted "extreme lens distortion" [14].

## How it is shot (stills and commercial work)

### The still photographer's method
- PRACTITIONER. Digital Camera World (Sian Lewis): to get both hands into a POV still, "hang your camera around your neck, switch to live view and compose the shot, then use the self-timer"; "At such close range a super wide-angle lens is a big help for getting everything in shot, but you could also use your kit lens at its widest zoom setting"; for sport, "Get hold of an SLR chest strap, which wraps around your waist and holds the camera snug against you"; for cycling, "Switch to shutter priority mode and pick 1/300 sec, then select the interval timer", aiming to "keep your hands and handlebars in focus while the rest of the scene blurs, creating a sense of speed"; subjects: "patting a dog, dangling over a wall or buying an ice cream" [23]. B&C Camera repeats the same recipe and adds the brief-writer's question: "Why do you want to show the world through your eyes?" [24]. Neither names a focal length in millimetres; neither says "tilt down" in words, but the neck-strap position with hands "dropped into position" below is a chest-height camera tilted at the hands (inference from [23][24]).
- STOCK EDITOR. Dreamstime (Tamara Bauer): first-person POV photos are "captured from eye level" and built from "foreground elements (hands, feet, props)"; the over-the-shoulder angle is offered as a related but distinct "up close and personal look"; gear is "GoPro, smartphone with tripod, camera with strap and interval timer", or "aiming with one hand while steadying the shot with the other"; uses named: "Editorial, travel, or lifestyle narratives", "Advertising, product, or service campaigns" [25]. 500px (Logan Bales): "A key to taking a successful first-person POV is incorporating hands, this not only adds to the illusion of being in a first-person POV, but the sense of touch and action"; feet work when "lying on a beach or sitting by a pool"; props can stand in for hands; buyers want images where viewers "place themselves within the scene" in "action-forward scenarios rather than passive viewing experience"; suggested subjects include "using tech devices" and "making dinner" [26].
- INDEX (what the commercial world calls it). Getty's controlled keyword is "Personal Perspective": 43,228 results, with related searches "personal perspective or pov", "personal perspective hands", "personal perspective coffee", "walking personal perspective", "personal perspective holding hands", "driving personal perspective" [31]. iStock: "Pov Hands" 60,800+ results, related "pov hands holding", "pov hands door", "pov hands holding box" [32]; "First Person View Hands" 42,500+, related "first person perspective" [33]; and, exactly our bed frame, "Pov Of Person In Bed Looking At Mobile Phone" 180,400+ results, tagged "Point of View", "Lying Down", "Holding", "Device Screen", "Selfie", "Using Phone" [34]. These are caption words attached to hundreds of thousands of licensed images, which is the strongest evidence available of what phrasing an image model has seen paired with this composition (inference on the training link; the counts are the pages' own).
- AD TEST. Right Left Agency ran identical Facebook ads for a fictional "Savi Bar", "only differing in the POV of the product photo"; the first-person version got "9% higher clicks (185 vs. 170), and 43% higher total engagement (n=32 vs. n=14)" and "Facebook's algorithm decreased the cost-per-click for the first-person ad by day three"; "Popular brands such as Google, Apple, and GoPro have used first-person imagery in their recent advertising campaigns" [27]. Small numbers; treat as directional.
- PRODUCTION COMPANY. Skystorm: "Shooting in POV often requires specialized head rigs, GoPros, or body cams"; "A blink, a whip pan, or a quick tilt can hide a cut"; the cost is that you lose "facial expressions or traditional over-the-shoulder shots" and "The entire emotional burden often falls on sound design, pacing, and movement" [28]. For a still, that burden lands on the hands and the environment (inference).
- THE PULLED-FORWARD FRAME HAS A NAME. Murad Osmann's #FollowMeTo series. Deborah Krieger (LA Review of Books) on the standard composition: "Her arm extends behind her, reaching toward the photographer and thus also toward the viewer. She grasps the photographer's outstretched left hand while his right hand ostensibly holds the camera, capturing the scene", which positions the viewer as the one being led rather than an onlooker [29]. The series began in Barcelona in 2011 when Natalia Zakharova "would pull him away, but he took the photograph anyway"; "Originally all #Followmeto photos were made on an iPhone, but recently Osmann is using a DSLR camera to have a wider field of view" [30]. Lesson: our reference frame (e) is this composition, and even its author moved to a wider lens to fit the arm and the scene.

### Where the camera sits on the body (mounts), and what each height shows
- MANUFACTURER. GoPro Head Strap: "perfect POV wherever you look", worn "directly on your head in a headlamp-like position" [36]. GoPro Chesty: "shoot immersive, hands-free POV footage from your chest" [35]; the Chesty page does not itself say what enters the frame. Retailer Coolblue fills that in: head strap, "Because the camera is higher than your eyes, you film from a higher perspective"; chest harness, "you record what's in front of you, but also your arms and legs", at "a lower viewing angle than with the Headstrap" [44].
- The ladder, top to bottom (assembled from [44][14][45][46]; the ordering is inference):
  - Forehead / head strap: above the eyes; when you bend forward it "will capture only half of your body" [14].
  - Eye level / glasses: Ray-Ban Meta glasses "let you snap a photo or video clip from your unique point of view" with "A new ultra-wide 12 MP camera" [46]; Axon offers "a Flex POV camera that can be attached to the eyeglasses or helmet" to align the camera with the eyes [45].
  - Mouth / chin (Hardcore Henry): "see the main body from the shoulders and chest" [14].
  - Chest (Chesty, body-worn camera): arms and legs in shot [44]; Axon Body 4 has "about 160-degree diagonal FOV"; in 16:9 "its horizontal FOV roughly matches the human stereo field of view (140 degrees), but the vertical FOV (76 degrees) is significantly narrower"; in 4:3 "127-degree horizontal and 93-degree vertical"; "Our stereo vision, where both eyes' fields overlap, covers about 120-140 degrees horizontally"; and "the body camera and the human eye are not perfectly aligned. We observe our surroundings by moving our head and eyes" [45].
- What this means for a still: a level camera at eye height gives the widest, most honest "my eyes" view; every step down the body adds more of one's own body at the bottom of the frame and lowers the viewpoint; the chest position is the one that reliably shows the wearer's own arms doing things [14][44]. Our reference frames (a) and (c) are chin-to-chest height tilted down; (b) is eye level tilted up at the ceiling; (d) and (e) are eye level, roughly level (inference from the frames and [14][44]).

### Lenses: what a 13 mm, 24 mm and 35 mm POV frame looks like
- MANUFACTURER, Apple. iPhone 17 rear cameras: "48MP Fusion Main: 26 mm, ƒ/1.6 aperture" and "48MP Fusion Ultra Wide: 13 mm, ƒ/2.2 aperture and 120° field of view", plus "12MP optical-quality 2x Telephoto: 52 mm"; the range is "2x optical zoom in, 2x optical zoom out" [47]. In the Camera app the Ultra Wide is the ".5x" button: "Tap .5x to switch to the Ultra Wide camera" [48]. So the phone in the reference frames, if it were the camera, would be shooting at 13 mm / 120° in 0.5x, or 26 mm in 1x.
- Angle of view on 35 mm full frame (diagonal / horizontal / vertical): 12 mm 122.0° / 111.1° / 90.0°; 14 mm 114.2° / 102.7° / 81.2°; 16 mm 107.1° / 95.1° / 73.9°; 24 mm 84.1° / 73.7° / 53.1°; 35 mm 63.4° / 54.4° / 37.8°; 50 mm 46.8° / 39.6° / 27.0° [49]. There is no 13 mm row; Apple's 120° figure sits between the 12 mm and 14 mm rows, so treat "13 mm" and "about 120° diagonal" as the same claim (inference from [47][49]).
- What that does at arm's length (computed from the horizontal angles in [49]; the distances are ours): at 50 cm from the lens a 35 mm frame spans about 51 cm across, a 24 mm frame about 75 cm, a 12 to 13 mm frame about 145 cm. Two hands held apart in front of the body fill a 35 mm frame edge to edge with no room left for the table or the street; they sit inside a 24 mm frame with the surroundings visible at the sides; in a 13 mm frame they occupy roughly the middle third and the whole room, ceiling included, wraps around them. That is why the phone ultra-wide and the GoPro are the native first-person lenses: the arm is short and the world is wide (inference; the geometry is arithmetic on [49]).
- MANUFACTURER, GoPro. The HERO12 sensor captures "a wide 156° field of view in a full-frame 8:7 aspect ratio" [38]; the Max Lens Mod 2.0 gives "the widest, tallest and most thrilling POV shots possible", "an ultra wide, industry-leading 177° field of view" [39]; the HERO13 Ultra Wide Lens Mod is "36% wider and 48% taller than shots captured with its standard lens", "makes the video look faster and more immersive", and the Ultra Wide Edition is sold as "the most stable, immersive 'put-you-in-the-moment' POV videos" [40][41]. Digital lenses: SuperView "takes the full readout from the 4:3 sensor and dynamically stretches it into a traditional 16:9 video" and is "a very immersive video, perfect for first-person POV"; Linear does "an incredible job of eliminating any fisheye effects" [37]; GoPro's press release says the 4:3 setting "utilizes the sensor's full height and width" and is "ideal for capturing: Ultra-immersive first-person perspectives that make you and your audience feel 'like you're there'" [42]. GoPro creative director Abe Kislevitz: Wide "is the standard fisheye distortion of the GoPro lens"; Linear is "An undistorted, non fish-eye view with straight lines. Linear will appear quite a bit more narrow than the wide lens, where wide has a 30% wider view than Linear"; SuperView is "created by taking the Wide Lens view on a 4:3 frame and squishing it down to fit inside a 16:9 frame" [43]. Two things transfer to stills: the first-person look is TALL (GoPro sells height, 48% taller, 4:3, 8:7, because the body is below the eyeline), and fisheye versus rectilinear is a choice, not a given [37][40][42][43] (transfer is inference).
- GoPro's per-lens degree tables ("HERO13 Black: Digital Lenses FOV Information" and the HERO12 equivalent) exist on community.gopro.com but the pages did not render through any fetch; third-party figures seen in search snippets (SuperView about 170°, Wide about 130°, Linear about 90°; 35 mm equivalents from about 12 mm for SuperView to about 39 mm for Linear) are UNVERIFIED and not used.

### Why the wide lens also makes the hands look right: foreshortening and edge stretch
- Foreshortening (Tate): "the technique of depicting an object or human body in a picture so as to produce an illusion of projection or extension in space", the distortion "seen by the eye when an object or figure is viewed at a distance or at an unusual angle" [53]. An arm pointing at the lens is the textbook case: the hand is large, the forearm short (inference from [53]).
- Distance does the work, not the glass. Jim Kasson: "Perspective magnification is proportional to 1 / Z"; edge objects stretch because "Surfaces at the edges are typically tilted relative to the camera. If you look straight at a circle on a wall, you see a circle. If you look at the same circle from the side, it becomes an ellipse" and because "Objects near the edges are often physically closer to the camera"; "The stretching is the world, faithfully projected. A rectilinear wide-angle lens is not distorting the world" [50]. Amateur Photographer names the effect: "'volume anamorphosis', a distortion that causes objects at the edge of the frame of a wideangle lens to become stretched compared to those in the centre"; people at the frame edge "widen in a way that no one is going to like" [51]. Bordwell and Thompson's wide-angle definition says the same in glossary form: "distorting straight lines near the edges of the frame and by exaggerating the distance between foreground and background planes" [1].
- A number for arm's length: Paskhover and Fried (JAMA Facial Plastic Surgery) modelled that a selfie "taken about 12 inches from the face" "makes the nasal base appear approximately 30 percent wider and the nasal tip 7 percent wider" than a photo at five feet [52]. Same physics as a hand 30 cm from the eyes: the near hand and the phone it holds will be visibly larger than the far hand, and a hand pushed into a corner of a 13 mm frame will widen (inference from [50][51][52]).

### Where the horizon sits
- The horizon line is the viewer's eye level: "It is the actual height of the viewer's eyes when looking at an object", and "Both horizon line and eye level lines are always perfectly horizontal" [55]; "The lines of the bricks that are below your eye level will be angled upwards, the ones at your eye level will be straight across, and the ones above your eye level will be angled downwards towards the eye level" [54].
- Film terms for the camera's pitch: angle of framing is "above it, looking down (a high angle); horizontal, on the same level (a straight-on-angle); below it, looking up (a low angle)", and a tilt is the camera "swiveling upward or downward on a stationary support" [1]; "The camera swivels up or down along its vertical axis" [2].
- Applied to our frames (inference from [54][55][1]): a level eye-height view puts the horizon, or its indoor equivalent, across the middle of the frame (frames d and e); tilting down toward the hands lifts the eye-level line toward the top edge and, at a steep tilt, out of frame altogether, so tabletops and floors show their tops and recede (frames a and c); tilting up from a pillow drops it below the bottom edge, so the ceiling, wall tops and the undersides of shelves show (frame b). Verticals converge downward in the tilted-down frames and upward in the tilted-up one; this is not a lens fault, it is how the model should know which way we are looking.

| SnorriCam / body mount | camera rigged to the actor "facing the actor directly" | the opposite of POV; never write "body-mounted" without saying where the lens looks | 21 |
| eyeline match / glance shot | the cut that certifies a POV in film | not usable in a still, but explains why the still must carry the body cue | 1, 2 |

## What separates a convincing result from a generic one

Concrete, checkable cues, each tied to a source.

1. The body enters from the BOTTOM edge, not from the sides. Arms "at the side of the camera" [6], "the main body from the shoulders and chest" [14], "your arms and legs" [44], "a hand reaching toward a door knob... lifting a glass" [13]. A hand entering from the left or right edge reads as someone else's hand, or as a third-person product shot (inference from [14][21]).
2. The near hand is larger than the far hand, and the forearm is short. That is foreshortening at arm's length [53] plus 1/Z magnification [50]; the selfie study's 30 percent at 12 inches is the scale of the effect [52]. A generic render draws both hands the same size, as if photographed from across the table.
3. The horizon is where the tilt says it is. Level: mid-frame. Looking down at hands: eye-level line at or above the top edge, floors and tabletops receding, verticals converging downward. Lying back: eye-level line below the bottom edge, ceiling and wall tops visible [54][55][1]. A generic render keeps a level horizon behind a tilted-down hand, which is a composite, not a view.
4. No head, no face, no shoulders seen from behind. Reflections are the only allowed face [13]; head-and-shoulders in frame is Noé's dream register, not eyes [16]; over-the-shoulder is a distinct stock category [25][26].
5. Other people, if present, look into the lens or at what the hands hold [9][13][18]. In frame (e) the leader's back is to the camera and her arm reaches back to the viewer's own hand [29].
6. The lens is wide and the edges say so. 13 mm / 120° on a phone [47], 156° to 177° on an action camera [38][40]; hands near the corners widen (volume anamorphosis) [51], straight lines stay straight on a phone (rectilinear) but bow on a GoPro Wide [43]. A generic render uses a 50 mm-looking flat perspective in which the room does not wrap around the hands.
7. Vertical or 4:3 proportions read more first-person than 16:9, because the body is below the eyeline: GoPro sells "48% taller" and the 4:3 mode for "Ultra-immersive first-person perspectives" [40][42]; Axon's 4:3 mode trades horizontal for 93° vertical [45]. For stills, a portrait or 4:3 crop keeps hands, phone and knees in the same frame (inference).
8. Something is moving or has just moved. Handheld unsteadiness [9][12], "heavy breathing and plodding footsteps" [19]; the still-photo version is directional blur in the floor and the far field while the hands stay sharp at 1/300 s [23]. Frames (d) and (e) need this; (a), (b) and (c) need stillness with a hint of hand softness at most.
9. An occluder or a near object crossing the edge: door edge, curtain, mask, the phone itself [12][13][19]. It certifies that the camera is inside the scene, not observing it.
10. The camera hand problem. If the person is holding a phone, the phone is the object in the hands, not the camera; the "eyes" are the camera. Stock POV shots are made by a neck-strap or chest-strap camera with self-timer precisely so both hands can be busy [23][25]. A generic render often shows one hand holding a phone "at" the viewer, which is a selfie, not a POV (inference from [23][25][48]).
11. The task is legible: "patting a dog... buying an ice cream" [23], "using tech devices", "making dinner" [26], Pryor's cigarette and glass [13]. Hands that hold nothing and do nothing are the generic failure; the craft's own subjects are all verbs (inference from the lists in [13][23][26]).

## Proposed clause upgrades

Each clause is written to be dropped into a prompt; the rationale says what it buys and which source it rests on. Combine one from each group; never stack a body-position clause with a contradictory tilt clause.

1. Framing declaration. "First-person point-of-view still, subjective camera: the camera is the person's own eyes, at their eye height; their head and face are not in the frame." Rationale: the glossary definition in its own words, plus the no-head rule; "subjective camera" carries the handheld connotation [1][5][16].
2. Body evidence. "Their own hands and forearms enter from the bottom edge of the frame, foreshortened, the nearer hand visibly larger than the farther one." Rationale: bottom-edge entry is the constant across Napoléon, Lady in the Lake, Hardcore Henry and chest-mount footage; foreshortening names the size difference [6][13][14][44][53].
3. Tilt, looking down (frames a and c). "Camera pitched down toward the hands from chin height, so the eye-level line is above the top of the frame; the tabletop / floor recedes below and verticals converge downward." Rationale: Hardcore Henry's chin position plus the horizon-equals-eye-level rule [14][54][55][1].
4. Tilt, lying back (frame b). "Lying on their back, phone held up above the face in both hands, arms rising from the bottom edge toward the ceiling; the wall and posters beyond, eye-level line below the frame." Rationale: iStock's "POV of person in bed looking at mobile phone" is a 180,400-image category, so the phrasing is native to the corpus; the horizon rule fixes the ceiling view [34][54][55].
5. Level walk (frames d and e). "Eye-height, level view, horizon across the middle of the frame, walking forward; slight handheld unsteadiness, directional motion blur in the floor and the far field while the hands stay sharp." Rationale: Blain Brown's "not too steady", No Film School's "natural bumpiness", the 1/300 s hands-sharp recipe [9][12][23].
6. The pulled-forward frame (e). "Their own outstretched arm reaches forward from the bottom edge, hand held by a companion whose back is to the camera, leading into the scene (the #FollowMeTo composition), ultra-wide." Rationale: Krieger's description of Osmann's series is the exact geometry; Osmann moved to a wider lens to fit it [29][30].
7. Lens, phone-native. "Ultra-wide 13 mm equivalent, 120° field of view, rectilinear (straight lines stay straight), hands near the frame corners slightly stretched." Rationale: Apple's spec phrase; the rectilinear qualifier stops GoPro fisheye; naming the corner stretch pre-empts the model 'fixing' it into a flat perspective [47][49][50][51].
8. Lens, calmer alternative (frames a and b). "Wide 24 mm equivalent, about 84° diagonal, mild wide-angle perspective." Rationale: for the breakfast table and the bed the world need not wrap; 24 mm keeps both hands and the tablet in frame with less corner distortion [49].
9. Aspect. "Portrait or 4:3 frame so the hands, the phone and the body below the eyeline stay in shot." Rationale: GoPro's "48% taller" and 4:3 "first-person perspectives" claims, Axon's 93° vertical in 4:3 [40][42][45].
10. What the hands hold. "The phone is the object in their hands, screen toward them, held at arm's length; the camera is their eyes, not the phone." Rationale: prevents the selfie reading; matches the neck-strap / self-timer method where both hands are free to act [23][25][48].
11. Exclusions. "Not a selfie, not over-the-shoulder, not a body-mounted camera facing the person; no face, no head, no shoulders from behind." Rationale: names the three neighbouring categories the corpus contains so the model does not slide into them [21][25][26].
12. Occluder (optional). "A door edge / the suitcase handle / the mug rim crosses the bottom of the frame close to the lens, out of focus." Rationale: Pryor's "a door moving toward you" and No Film School's branches and curtains; certifies the camera is inside the action [12][13].
13. Task verb. "Hands doing one legible thing: lifting the mug, thumb on the phone edge, gripping the suitcase handle." Rationale: every practitioner list of POV subjects is a list of actions; hands that hold nothing are the generic failure [13][23][26].

Vocabulary to avoid or qualify: "POV" alone (fine, but pair it with "first-person" and "own hands" since "POV" is also a social-caption word, inference); "body-mounted" (SnorriCam risk [21]); "GoPro" or "SuperView" unless we want fisheye curvature and the 16:9 stretch [37][43]; "viewmodel" (pulls toward game rendering [56][58]).
