# Schedule POV, overhead and motion: research dossier
_Researched 2026-09-15. Sources listed at the bottom, numbered; cite by number inline._

Scope: two reference compositions for the Schedule (scheduled-delivery) image set, where the person is in an Indian city and their own hands enter the frame holding a phone or doing a task. Part A is the Wes Anderson style true overhead (camera straight down at a table, hands from the bottom edge). Part B is the walking, pulled-by-the-hand, ultra-wide phone POV with motion blur, plus the suitcase and passenger-seat variants. Everything not marked "(inference)" or UNVERIFIED was read on the cited page. Companion file: `schedule-images-phone-and-density.md` already flags "POV-down-the-body and true overhead as high-risk" angles that want a reference image; this dossier supplies the vocabulary for exactly those two.

## Part A: the planimetric overhead

### What the composition actually is

**The film term is Bordwell's "planimetric" staging, and the overhead is that idea rotated onto a table.** David Bordwell's definition: "The camera stands perpendicular to a rear surface, usually a wall. The characters are strung across the frame like clothes on a line." His early working term was "mug-shot framing"; the word "planimetric" (or "planar") he borrowed from art historian Heinrich Wölfflin; "such imagery is quite rare before, say, 1960" [1]. He names the lineage: Fassbinder, Antonioni, Godard, Hou Hsiao-hsien, Angelopoulos, Kitano, Kore-eda, Terence Davies, and among Americans Wes Anderson, Todd Haynes and Jared Hess [1]. Its companion is "compass-point editing": cuts that switch the camera by 90 or 180 degrees, or hold the same 0-degree axis, instead of classical over-the-shoulder reverse angles [1][2]. The functions he lists: stasis, passivity, childish simplicity, oppression, the look of posed photography, deadpan comedy [1].

In the 2014 Grand Budapest essay he restates it as: "you frame people against a perpendicular background, as if they were in a police line-up"; the result is "a somewhat awkward formality, a sense that we are looking from a distance into an enclosed world that sometimes looks back at us"; Anderson's "dollhouse-room frames make adults seem to be toy people arranged just so, like items laid out in a Joseph Cornell box"; practitioners of the approach "often deploy symmetry" [2]. Wikipedia's summary of the style, for the record: "flat space camera moves (pans, tilts, and zooms within scenes that look two-dimensional), symmetrical compositions", a "scale-model household" [11].

**The phrase "shooting the flat".** Not found verbatim on any of the three Bordwell pages read [1][2][3]. What Bordwell actually writes is "planimetric", "planar", "mug-shot framing" and "police line-up". Treat "shooting the flat" as UNVERIFIED as a Bordwell coinage; do not put it in a prompt.

**The sentence that defines the overhead insert.** Bordwell, on how Anderson shows props: "In other films, the notes and maps and book passages that characters examine would be presented in a naturalistic way, clutched in hands or seen through optical POV. Instead Anderson simply thrusts these things at us, perfectly framed and lit, like items in a gallery display." [3]. That is the whole distinction the Schedule brief needs: the overhead is a gallery display of the object, not the character's eyes.

**The Kogonada supercut.** "Wes Anderson // From Above", Kogonada, 2012 [5]. The one-line description carried by a contemporaneous repost: "Overhead shots of hands in the films of Wes Anderson." [7]. The Culturist called it "a collage of overhead shots of hands taken from films by Wes Anderson" [6]; Open Culture describes the essay as being about Anderson "composing shots from directly overhead" and pairs it with Kogonada's "Centered" essay on frames where "the most important thing is smack in the middle" [8]. Music per Kogonada's own portfolio page: "High-Speed French Train" by Alexandre Desplat [4]. (A search snippet from an unfetched fan wiki credits a different track, "More to See" by Jonathan Elias; UNVERIFIED, possibly two cuts of the video.) The USC Film Studies in Motion page classes it as a supercut with "an implicit thesis" and "no works cited list" [5]. Wes Anderson's reported reaction in a 2012 Vulture interview ("all I could see was how many hours of setup time went into all of those shots") appeared only in search snippets; vulture.com blocks the crawler and the fan wiki returned 402, so the quote is UNVERIFIED.

**How Anderson and Yeoman shoot.** Bordwell: Anderson favours wide-angle lenses, "often 40mm", where other planimetric directors use long lenses; "These make horizontal lines bulge" [2]. Bordwell also notes the long-lens version of the style gives "flatter-looking, more cardboardy space" [1], so Anderson's wide lens is the exception, and it is why his overheads show a hint of curvature at the table edges (inference from [1][2]). Robert Yeoman, via Cooke Optics: Bottle Rocket was shot almost entirely on one 27mm Primo; on Rushmore and The Royal Tenenbaums "at least 90% was shot on a single 40mm anamorphic lens"; the camera assistants measure from the lens to the room corners to guarantee dead-centre placement, "With Wes that's just kind of a given"; deep focus throughout, stopping down to T4 interiors and T11 exteriors; Cooke S4 primes for spherical work [9]. No Film School confirms the audience association: "overheads of perfectly placed props" are one of the three things people attach to Anderson (with whip pans and slow-motion tracking) [10]. Yeoman on 4:3 in Grand Budapest: "we often gave people a lot more headroom than is customary" [2]. **The rig for the overheads: nothing solid found.** Neither the Cooke piece nor No Film School describes how the top-down inserts are mounted, and the ASC Moonrise Kingdom article returned 403.

**What the film glossaries call the angle.** StudioBinder: "An overhead shot is from above, looking down on your subject. These are typically shot from 90 degrees above, anything less might be considered a high angle shot instead." [12]. Morphic's glossary separates three terms: God's eye view is "precisely vertical" at 90 degrees with "no horizon in frame"; bird's eye view "only implies height, so it can stay tilted and keep some depth or a distant skyline"; and "overhead is the neutral term for a top-down frame", used for "food, product, and process shots", while God's eye names the same geometry when the height does narrative work, reading as "fate, surveillance, or judgement" [13]. Vimeo's blog: a God's-eye view "captures an overhead angle by placing the camera directly above the subject", so that "objects in the frame may be unrecognizable at first"; Hitchcock is the named user [14]. No Film School lists the synonyms in circulation: God's eye view, bird's eye view, raised shot, elevated shot, and defines overhead as "almost directly (or literally directly) above the subject" [15].

**What kind of shot it is: an insert.** StudioBinder: "An insert shot is a close-up that isolates a specific object, detail, or action within a scene"; inserts are "typically shot from the point of view of a character, matching their eyeline" [16]. Wikipedia: an insert is a close-up "of part of a scene filmed from a different angle and/or focal length than the master shot", usually of objects or body parts excluding the head, "often done separately from the main action, by a second-unit director using stand-ins", and it must hold continuity by "keeping the objects in the same relative position as in the main take, and having the lighting be the same" [17]. So the Anderson overhead is an insert that deliberately refuses the eyeline rule in [16]: the hands are the character's, the viewpoint is not (inference, consistent with Bordwell's "gallery display" versus "optical POV" [3]).

**What still photographers call it: flat lay, and the discipline behind it, knolling.** Replica Surfaces' rule: "position your camera so the lens is pointing straight down, perpendicular to your photography surface", giving "a 90-degree angle between your camera's sensor plane and the surface plane"; "When your camera is perfectly parallel to your surface, every part of the scene is an equal distance from the lens", which is what prevents keystoning; "an angle between 85 and 90 degrees will appear distortion-free to the viewer"; stepping back to "about 45 degrees above the surface includes more of the environment" and is the lifestyle angle for hands and context; 0 to 30 degrees "will significantly distort"; "Wide-angle lenses (like a 24mm) are more prone to barrel distortion, especially at the edges of the frame" and "a mild telephoto lens (50mm or above) is preferable"; use the grid overlay and the electronic level to check [20]. Lars Miller Media: 35 to 50mm, "a 50mm prime lens on a full-frame camera is the sweet spot", "the camera is pointed straight down, perfectly parallel to the flat surface. This eliminates perspective distortion", supported by "a tripod with a horizontal center column (also called a boom arm)" or a copy stand [23]. Knolling: "the practice of organizing objects in parallel or at 90° angles", popularised by Tom Sachs, who learned it from Andrew Kromelow at Frank Gehry's furniture shop, named after Knoll [18]; the working rule: "Arrange each group so every edge is either parallel or perpendicular to every other edge. Objects face the same direction. Nothing overlaps.", and the result "is a flat-lay grid" [19].

**Light.** Flat-lay practice defaults to soft light: "Avoid direct sunlight, which creates harsh, high-contrast shadows"; "A larger light source placed close to your set will produce softer shadows"; and, critically for the geometry, "Any angle slightly off 90 degrees will create skewed, elongated shadows" [21]. But shadows are kept: "a complete absence of shadows can make an image look two-dimensional and sterile"; "The goal isn't always to eliminate them completely, but to understand, control, and use them to add dimension, depth, and mood" [21]. The hard-light alternative is a deliberate stylist's choice: "The bigger the light source, the softer the light"; hard light "comes from small or distant sources like direct sunlight", with sharp shadow edges and deep shadows, used "strategically and creatively" [22]. Studio recipe: two softboxes at 45 degrees on opposite sides angled down, "equal power on both lights for even illumination (adjust one light slightly lower for subtle dimension)", diffusion "of at least 24 inches", 5000K to 5500K [23]. Anderson's overheads are "perfectly framed and lit" [3]; that they read as flat and even is an observation from the reference grid in the brief, not a sourced claim.

### True overhead versus first-person tilt-down

The two are routinely confused in prompts and they are different cameras. The table states what each source pins down.

| Axis | TRUE overhead (Anderson / flat lay) | FIRST-PERSON tilt-down (the person's own eyes) |
|---|---|---|
| Where the camera is | Above the table, lens pointing straight down at 90 degrees, sensor plane parallel to the surface [12][20][23] | "where a character's eyes would be" [34]; head or chest height, tilted down toward the hands (inference from [34][36]) |
| Whose view it is | Nobody's: "like items in a gallery display", explicitly not "optical POV" [3]; an insert that ignores the eyeline rule [16][17] | The character's; "showing what they see" [33][34] |
| Where the hands enter | From the bottom edge, forearms roughly parallel to the frame sides (the supercut is "overhead shots of hands" [7]; the parallel-forearm detail is inference from the 90-degree geometry) | From the bottom corners, converging toward the centre as the arms come from the shoulders (inference); needs a wide lens: "A 50mm is too tight for the operator to use his/her own hands for the shot", 14mm to 35mm recommended [35]; "a super wide-angle lens is beneficial for capturing everything in the frame when working at such close range" [36] |
| Horizon | None in frame [13] | Just above the top edge or in the top band, depending on tilt (inference); the 45-degree lifestyle angle "includes more of the environment" [20] |
| Perspective | Flat: "every part of the scene is an equal distance from the lens" [20]; at 40mm, table edges bulge slightly [2] | Keystoned: off-perpendicular angles produce visible distortion, and near objects (the phone, the near hand) look large relative to far ones [20][38] |
| Shadows | Short, under the objects, soft-edged if the source is large [21][22] | Cast away from the light, longer, and the person's own head and shoulders can shade the table (inference) |
| Lens | 40mm (Anderson) [2][9]; 35 to 50mm, ideally 50mm (flat lay) [20][23] | Wide: 14 to 35mm [35]; on a phone, the 24mm main or 13mm ultra-wide [29][30] |
| What to call it | "true overhead", "camera directly above, pointing straight down", "flat lay", "God's-eye view" only if the mood is surveillance [12][13][20] | "POV shot", "first-person", "subjective camera" [33][34] |

The middle case, the camera raised "about 45 degrees above the surface", is a third thing: neither overhead nor POV, the standard lifestyle angle for hands and context [20]. Name whichever of the three you mean; never write "overhead POV".

## Part B: the walking and pulled-by-the-hand POV

### What the composition actually is

**#FollowMeTo (Murad Osmann and Nataly Osmann, from 2011).** The origin, in Nataly's words: on their first trip to Barcelona in 2011, "I wanted to show him the old town part, grabbed his arm and dragged him forward and, as you can understand, it didn't stop him from taking a picture even then" [26]; "It started in Barcelona, it was our first trip together. We took our first picture there, then another one, and it went on and on" [27]. The formula: "Nataly leading the way, Murad at arm's length", Nataly in the foreground pulling him along [26]. Wikipedia: she "would pull him away, but he took the photograph anyway"; "all #Followmeto photos were made on an iPhone" at first, and later "Osmann is using a DSLR camera to have a wider field of view" [24]. It went from spontaneous to planned: "We do prepare more and plan ahead, we choose the locations and dresses" [27]; a behind-the-scenes account, @followmetraveller, and a YouTube video ("#FollowMeTo India by Murad and Nataly Osmann") show the process [28].

**Osmann's own gear notes (primary).** "Our first pictures for the #FollowMeTo series were made on the iPhone camera." Then a Canon with a 16-35: "My favorite lens is 16/35 with 2.8 lens aperture", stopped down to smaller apertures (the page gives them as 7 to 8, which reads as f/7 to f/8, inference) "to keep the arm, Natasha, and background in focus". "I use 16mm or 14mm lenses", and an 11mm for a helicopter frame over Los Angeles. He tested the Leica Q and found its fixed 28mm too tight for the series' framing; the Leica M appealed because "it has 16mm, 18mm, and 21mm lenses!" Bodies: Canon 5D Mark II, 5D, 5DS, Sony Alpha 7 and 7R [25]. Elsewhere: "We use regular high-quality cameras like Canon Mark III or Sony Alpha a7R or Nikon D800 with wide-angle lenses" [26]. Two facts to carry into prompts: the lens is 14 to 16mm on full frame, which is the same class as a phone's 13mm ultra-wide [29]; and the whole chain, arm, partner and background, is in focus, not a shallow portrait look [25].

**Composition, stated as geometry.** The camera is in one of the photographer's hands at arm's length and the other arm is held by the partner [24][26]. That puts the photographer's forearm entering from a bottom corner and running to the partner's hand around mid-frame, the partner a step or two ahead with her back to the camera, and the destination beyond her (inference from "at arm's length" [26] and the 14 to 16mm focal lengths [25], which are what you need to get your own arm and the far scene into one frame [35][36]). Nobody's face is in it, which is why it maps so directly onto the faceless Schedule brief.

**Film vocabulary for the POV itself.** Wikipedia: a point-of-view shot (also "subjective camera", "first-person shot") is one where "the camera is positioned to show what a certain character is seeing from their perspective"; it needs "supporting narrative elements" (shot sequencing, sound, acting) to read as POV; Lady in the Lake (1947) is the whole-film example [33]. StudioBinder: "A POV (point-of-view) shot places the camera where a character's eyes would be, showing what they see"; "Use a handheld or Steadicam setup to mimic natural head movements" [34]. Practitioner rules for getting your own hands in: a 50mm "is too tight"; 14mm to 35mm; Being John Malkovich harnessed a Moviecam to Malkovich's shoulder with an 18mm or 14mm; have "the camera operator be the hand/feet in the shot" [35]. Still-photo version: wear the camera on a chest strap so it "moves as your body moves"; "a super wide-angle lens is beneficial for capturing everything in the frame when working at such close range"; for hands, hang the camera round your neck, compose on live view, self-timer, then drop your hands into position; for a speed effect while cycling, "shutter priority mode with a 1/300 second exposure time" keeps "your hands and handlebars" sharp while the background blurs [36]. Video creators say the same: chest harnesses sit "approximately five feet high" when standing; the action camera's "ultra-wide lens" gives "the classic POV look"; hands are the engagement device, "Point at landmarks. Touch surfaces. Hold items up to examine them." [37].

**The modern phone version: 0.5x.** Apple's own tech specs for iPhone 15 Pro: "12MP Ultra Wide: 13 mm, ƒ/2.2 aperture and 120° field of view", with "2x optical zoom out" from the 24mm main [29]. Moment's iPhone 17 breakdown: 0.5x Ultra Wide is 13mm, 1x is 24mm, 4x is 100mm; the ultra-wide "hasn't changed physically" in field of view and the macro mode is a crop of it [30]. Samsung's own buying guide: Galaxy S25 Ultra ultra-wide "50 MP F1.9", S25 and S25+ ultra-wide "12 MP F2.2"; Samsung's page does not state the field of view in degrees [31]. The widely reported 120-degree figure for Samsung's ultra-wide appeared only in search snippets; UNVERIFIED on a fetched page. GoPro, for the action-camera end of the same look: "156° field of view in full 8:7 aspect ratio", and 177 degrees with the Max Lens Mod 2.0, "the widest view available in a GoPro" [32]. Apple's "Lens Correction" camera setting (which straightens the ultra-wide's edges by default, per search snippets): the Apple support page is a JavaScript shell and could not be read; UNVERIFIED.

**What the ultra-wide does to the frame, in the right words.** Three separate effects, and prompts blur them:

1. Extension (wide-angle) perspective distortion, which is about distance, not glass: "Objects close to the lens appear abnormally large relative to more distant objects, and distant objects appear abnormally small and hence farther away"; "Linear perspective changes are caused by distance, not by the lens per se" [38]. In a pulled-by-the-hand frame the photographer's own forearm is centimetres from the lens, so it is huge; the partner a metre away is normal; the street is small and far (inference from [38]).
2. Volume deformation, also called volume anamorphosis, which is what stretches people at the edges: "caused by the way wide-angle lenses capture object volumes, particularly when the subjects appear along the edges of the image", because "photography uses only two dimensions to try to reproduce the three-dimensional world"; it takes two forms, the "big head syndrome" and the "too-wide syndrome" [39]. A practitioner's description at 24mm or wider: edge subjects "look kind of stretched, as if they were taller and skinnier than they should be" [40]. This is not lens distortion and survives full distortion correction (DxO's glossary framing [39]).
3. Barrel distortion proper, the bowing of straight lines toward the edges: "Wide-angle lenses (like a 24mm) are more prone to barrel distortion, especially at the edges of the frame" [20]. Phones correct most of it in software (UNVERIFIED for Apple, see above), so in a realistic phone still the lines are nearly straight but the corners are still stretched by effect 2 (inference).

**Motion blur: which kind, and how much.** Definitions from an image editor's manual, because those are the terms the generators learned: linear motion blur "creates a linear blur in a direction determined by the 'Angle' option", where "a setting of 90 will produce a vertical blur, and a setting of 0 will produce a horizontal blur" [50]; zoom blur "creates a blur in all directions around a center", a radial blur from a chosen centre [51]; circular (spin) blur "creates a circular blur around a center" [52]. Panning blur is the photographic case where the camera follows a moving subject: "The exposure time must be long enough to allow the background to blur due to the camera movement as the photographer follows the subject in the viewfinder"; the subject stays sharp, the background streaks horizontally; a running person at "as slow as 1/40 second", a Porsche at 1/125, F1 at 1/250 [42]. Tamron's ladder: 1/60 to 1/125 to start, then 1/30, then "1/15 seconds for maximum dynamism"; streaks run opposite to the subject's travel; pan by "rotating your entire upper body" [43]. Adorama: for panning, "Shutter speed between 1/15 and 1/60 sec (depending upon the speed of your subject)"; a static camera needs "half a second, a full second, or even longer" to blur a walking person (the same article elsewhere says 1/200 is enough for a person walking by; the two statements contradict, so use it only for the panning range) [41]. Rocky Nook: "shutter speeds of 1/30s and longer can generally be considered a slow shutter speed"; at 1/30 a runner leaves "a trail of blurred streaks" [44]. Street practice: "Speeds from 1/8th/s to 1/30th/s work best for me" [45]; Simon Burn settles on 1/8 s in overcast light at ISO 100 and f/16, and notes "When someone walks across the frame, the motion blur is more exaggerated than if they were walking toward or away from the camera", best "within about 10 to 15 feet of the camera" [46]. The handheld range that keeps "part of your frame sharp while letting motion streak through it" "sits between roughly a tenth of a second and a 125th" [47]. Intentional camera movement starts slower still, at 1/2 s, with 1/4 s for fast moves [49]. For the film-still look specifically, video's 180-degree rule gives 1/50 s at 24fps (1/48 ideal): faster shutters give "little to no motion blur" and look choppy, slower give "more pronounced motion blur" [48].

**So what is the walking POV, technically?** It is a panning shot turned inside out: the hand, forearm and phone travel with the camera, so they are the "subject" that stays sharp; the ground, the kerb and passers-by move relative to the camera and streak (inference from the panning principle in [42][43]). Forward walking is not a sideways pan, so the streaks are not horizontal: the world expands from the vanishing point, so blur is near zero at the point you are walking toward, and grows outward and downward, strongest on the pavement under the frame and at the side edges. That is zoom-blur geometry [51], not linear [50] and not spin [52] (inference, supported by Burn's observation that across-frame motion blurs more than toward-camera motion [46]). **The 1/15 to 1/30 s range in the brief:** no fetched source states it for a walking POV specifically. It sits inside every adjacent range that was read: panning 1/15 to 1/60 [41][43], the slow-shutter threshold at 1/30 [44], street 1/8 to 1/30 [45], and the handheld part-sharp band of 1/10 to 1/125 [47]; treat it as a well-supported inference, not a citation. The one direct hands-sharp POV number found is 1/300 s for cycling [36], which is a much faster subject.

**Walking with a suitcase or bag.** Nothing solid found as a named composition. Build it from the same rules: the person's own hand and the handle enter from a bottom corner (hands-in-frame practice [36][37]), wide lens [35], hand and handle sharp, floor streaking (the panning principle above).

**POV from a vehicle passenger seat.** Practitioner rules for shooting from a moving car: "Shooting 90 degrees out from the car maximizes the potential for image blur because of motion", while "If you shoot at, say, 45 degrees forward or backward, you have reduced the apparent speed of the vehicle significantly"; "Even with a shutter speed of 1/400th second, things close to the road may have motion blur"; shooting through the windscreen or rear glass "doesn't work" because of "glass angle and curvature"; "Get as close to the glass as you can to minimize reflections"; a polariser fails through tinted side glass, producing a grid of dark ovals [53]. The deliberate-blur version: "an aperture of f/16 and shutter speed 1/10 second" with the camera "rested on the dashboard to stabilize it", which turns the road into streaks while the car interior holds (the interior holding is inference from the panning principle; the numbers are [54]); small apertures reveal "every windshield imperfection and sensor dust" [54]. Framing: "The view through the windshield, side mirrors, or even from the sunroof can add an interesting element", and "Experiment with slower shutter speeds to create motion blur, either of the scenery whizzing by, or the blur of the road while keeping the horizon sharp" [55]. Translated to the Schedule brief: the dashboard, A-pillar and the person's own hand on the phone are the fixed frame; the side window carries the streaking city; the horizon in the windscreen stays readable; reflections on the glass are a realism cue, not a defect (inference from [53][54][55]).

## What separates a convincing result from a generic one

**Part A, the overhead.**
1. **Ninety degrees means ninety.** "Even 80 degrees introduces visible perspective distortion" is the flat-lay orthodoxy (85 to 90 passes) [20]; anything less and the table's far edge foreshortens, shadows elongate [21], and the frame becomes a high angle [12]. Write "straight down" and "sensor parallel to the tabletop", not "from above".
2. **It is nobody's eyes.** The convincing frame is Bordwell's gallery display [3]: object dead centre, hands as instruments, no head, no shoulders, no horizon [13][17]. The generic frame sneaks in a forehead, a hint of chest or a wall, which converts it into a tilt-down POV.
3. **Hands come from the bottom edge and do something.** The supercut is "overhead shots of hands" [7] and the insert convention is body parts handling an object [17]. Hands hovering, or entering from the sides, read as a stock flat lay with a model.
4. **Knolled, not scattered, not piled.** Parallel and perpendicular edges, nothing overlapping, all facing one way [19], with bilateral symmetry about the centre [2][8]. "Neatly arranged" gets you a tidy pile; "knolled" gets you the grid.
5. **Choose the lens on purpose.** Anderson's 40mm makes "horizontal lines bulge" [2]; the catalogue flat lay at 50mm has none of that [20][23]. Either is fine; the mix (a 13mm phone look on a table) is what makes AI overheads look wrong.
6. **Light is soft but shadows exist.** The known failure is the shadowless render, "two-dimensional and sterile" [21]. Ask for short, soft-edged shadows under the objects from a large diffused source [21][22][23]. If hard light, say so and accept sharp-edged shadows [22].
7. **Everything sharp.** Deep focus is the house style on both sides of the reference [9][23]; shallow focus with a blurry table edge is a phone-portrait default leaking in.

**Part B, the walking POV.**
1. **The whole chain is in focus.** Osmann stops down so "the arm, Natasha, and background" are all sharp [25]. A shallow-focus version with a bokeh street is a different, generic photo.
2. **Arm from a corner, hand mid-frame, partner's back.** "At arm's length" [26] with 14 to 16mm glass [25] puts the forearm in the bottom corner, very large, running to a hand near the centre, the partner one step ahead, no faces. If the arm comes from the bottom centre and looks normal-sized, the camera was not at the eye and the lens was not wide.
3. **Ultra-wide signatures, three of them, dosed separately.** Near objects huge, far small [38]; corners stretched [39][40]; lines nearly straight because the phone corrects barrel [20] (correction behaviour UNVERIFIED for Apple). Asking for "fisheye" over-delivers all three.
4. **Blur has a geometry.** Hand and phone sharp because they travel with the camera; pavement and edges streak radially from the vanishing point; the far end of the street nearly sharp [42][46][51] (inference). A uniform motion-blur wash, or horizontal smears on a forward walk, is the giveaway.
5. **Give the shutter as a number, and keep it modest.** 1/15 to 1/30 s sits in panning practice [41][43] and above the slow-shutter threshold [44]; 1/8 s and slower is street-abstract [45][46]; 1/2 s is ICM [49]. A film-still look is nearer 1/50 s [48].
6. **The vehicle frame is the interior.** Dashboard, A-pillar, the hand and phone are fixed and sharp; the side window carries the streaks (90 degrees to travel blurs most, 45 degrees forward less) [53]; roadside objects nearest the car blur even at fast shutters [53]; glass reflections are expected [53]. A windscreen full of horizontal streaks at 1/10 s is the wrong window [53][55].
7. **The image model already drifts on these two angles.** The phone-and-density dossier records that "Text prompts alone rarely produce a correct POV" and true overhead "tends to drift on extreme verticals", with a reference image as the fix; the clauses below are written to be used with a reference, not instead of one.

## Proposed clause upgrades

Each clause is written to drop into the Schedule prompt's camera slot. Rationale and sources follow each.

### Overhead set (Part A)

- **OV-1 Geometry.** "True overhead: the camera is directly above the table and points straight down at 90 degrees, sensor parallel to the tabletop, no horizon and no wall in frame." Rationale: the 90-degree rule and its tolerance [20]; the film glossary's 90-degree threshold [12]; God's-eye framing has "no horizon in frame" [13].
- **OV-2 Hands.** "The person's two hands enter from the bottom edge of the frame, forearms parallel, [doing the task: holding the phone flat over the table / unpacking the delivery box / ticking a list]; no head, face or shoulders anywhere in frame." Rationale: the supercut's content [7]; inserts show body parts excluding the head [17]; Bordwell's gallery display, not optical POV [3].
- **OV-3 Arrangement.** "Objects knolled on the table: grouped by kind, every edge parallel or perpendicular to the frame, nothing overlapping, all facing the same way, laid out with bilateral symmetry around the centre." Rationale: knolling rule verbatim [19]; symmetry as the planimetric habit [2][8].
- **OV-4 Lens, film variant.** "Shot on a 40mm lens, so the table edges bow very slightly outward at the frame edges; deep focus, everything sharp." Rationale: Anderson's 40mm and the bulge [2][9]; deep focus at T4 or T11 [9].
- **OV-4b Lens, catalogue variant.** "Shot on a 50mm lens from a tripod boom arm, table edges dead straight and parallel to the frame, no barrel distortion, everything sharp." Rationale: flat-lay lens practice and rigging [20][23].
- **OV-5 Light, soft.** "Soft, even light from one large diffused source above the table; short, soft-edged shadows directly under each object; no hard sun, no camera shadow." Rationale: large source close gives soft shadows, off-90 gives elongated shadows [21]; shadows kept for dimension [21]; softbox recipe [23]; the camera-shadow ban is inference from the camera being between light and table.
- **OV-5b Light, hard.** "One hard light from a high side angle throwing sharp-edged, short shadows across the tabletop." Rationale: the hard-light choice as a stylist's tool [22].
- **OV-6 Negative.** "Not a high angle, not a 45-degree lifestyle angle, not a bird's-eye view with a horizon, not the person's own point of view." Rationale: the three angles are distinct [12][13][20]; POV is the other composition [3][34].

### Walking and pulled-by-the-hand set (Part B)

- **WP-1 Camera.** "First-person point of view from the person's own eye height, the phone held out at arm's length in their right hand, shot on the phone's ultra-wide camera, 13 mm equivalent, 0.5x, 120-degree field of view." Rationale: POV is the camera at the eyes [33][34]; Apple's ultra-wide numbers [29][30]; Osmann's own 14 to 16mm as the equivalent on full frame [25].
- **WP-2 Arm and partner.** "The person's own left forearm enters from the bottom left corner, very large and close to the lens, and reaches forward to a companion's hand at the middle of the frame; the companion walks one step ahead with their back to the camera, leading; no faces." Rationale: the #FollowMeTo geometry, "at arm's length", partner leading [24][26]; the near-large forearm is extension distortion [38]; hands-in-frame needs the wide lens [35][36].
- **WP-3 Depth.** "Small aperture, deep depth of field: the forearm, the companion and the far end of the street are all in focus." Rationale: Osmann stops down to hold "the arm, Natasha, and background in focus" [25].
- **WP-4 Motion.** "Slow shutter of about 1/20 second while walking: the forearm, hand and phone stay sharp because they move with the camera; the pavement, kerb and passing people streak, the blur radiating outward from the vanishing point ahead and strongest at the frame edges and along the ground; the far end of the street almost sharp." Rationale: panning keeps the co-moving subject sharp [42][43]; 1/15 to 1/30 sits inside the panning [41][43], slow-shutter [44] and street [45] ranges (the exact number is inference); across-frame motion blurs more than toward-camera motion [46]; radial geometry is zoom blur, not linear or spin [50][51][52].
- **WP-5 Edges.** "Wide-angle stretch at the corners: objects at the frame edges pulled slightly wider and taller, straight lines almost straight but bowing a little at the extreme edges, verticals leaning if the phone tilts." Rationale: volume deformation at the edges [39][40]; barrel distortion on wide lenses [20]; converging verticals off-perpendicular [38].
- **WP-6 Horizon.** "Horizon in the upper third, the street receding to a vanishing point near the centre behind the companion." Rationale: inference; no fetched source specifies the #FollowMeTo horizon, and this placement follows from an eye-height camera on a wide lens tilted slightly down [34][36].
- **WP-7 Suitcase or bag variant.** "First-person, ultra-wide: the person's own right hand and the pull handle of a wheeled suitcase enter from the bottom right corner, hand and handle sharp, the floor streaking past below; the other hand holds the phone." Rationale: nothing solid found as a named composition; built from hands-in-frame practice [36][37], the wide-lens requirement [35] and the panning principle [42].
- **WP-8 Passenger seat.** "From the front passenger seat of a car: the dashboard, A-pillar and the person's own hand holding the phone frame the lower and right edges, sharp; through the side window the street streaks past in horizontal motion blur at about 1/15 second, nearest objects blurring most; through the windscreen the road ahead and the horizon stay readable; faint reflections of the interior on the side glass." Rationale: 90 degrees to travel blurs most, 45 degrees forward less [53]; near-road objects blur first [53]; dashboard-braced slow shutter [54]; road blur with a sharp horizon, windscreen framing [55]; reflections and glass curvature [53]; linear blur is the right kind for the side window [50].
- **WP-9 Negative.** "Not a panning shot of someone else, not a spin blur, not a fisheye, not a shallow-focus portrait, no face in frame." Rationale: spin blur is rotation [52]; fisheye over-delivers all three wide-angle effects [20][38][39]; deep focus is the signature [25].
