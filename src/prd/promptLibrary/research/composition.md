# Composition (FMCG product staging) · research dossier
_Researched 2026-08-15. Sources listed at the bottom, numbered; cite by number inline._

## What the medium actually is

Commercial product still life is a craft with a settled vocabulary. Almost every dial in this system (angle, colour, shadow, count, surface) maps onto a decision a real set photographer makes before the first frame. The prompts get better when they use the trade word rather than a description of the effect.

### Camera and angle

**Focal length.** The trade default for packshot work sits in the 50-100mm range, with macro capability [4]. Longer is safer: the longer the lens, the less perspective distortion, and 85-100mm lets you stand back far enough that the camera and rig do not cast into the set or disturb the styling [1]. The compression of a longer lens also reads as "product photography" rather than "phone photo" · the pack looks more three-dimensional and more separated from the background [1]. Practically, for a prompt, "shot on a 100mm macro at f/8" carries focal length, working distance and aperture in one clause.

**Viewpoint vs angle.** These are two separate axes in the trade taxonomy, and conflating them is a common prompt error [8]:
- Viewpoint (camera height): **low shot** (beneath the subject, establishes power and grandeur), **eye-level** (straight on, the ecommerce default), **high shot** (elevated, looking down) [8].
- Angle (which face of the product you see): **front / straight-on** (default ecommerce hero, best for packaging that stands up), **profile**, **back angle** (label, nutrition panel, ingredients · a trust shot), **45-degree** (also called the **three-quarter**, shows two faces at once, mimics how you see an object on a table), **top angle / bird's-eye** (flat lay), **macro** (fine detail, texture, print quality on the pack) [8].

So "high 45" is really viewpoint=high + angle=three-quarter. "Hero low angle" is viewpoint=low + angle=front-or-three-quarter, and its meaning in the category is premium, grand, authoritative [8].

**Parallel verticals.** Product and architectural photographers care about the same thing: when you tip the camera up or down at an upright object, verticals converge and you get the **keystone effect**. A **tilt-shift** (perspective-control) lens shifts the lens element relative to the sensor instead of tipping the body, so verticals stay parallel and a bottle or carton keeps its true shape [9]. Shifting also lets you shoot near-front-on to a glossy pack without the camera appearing in the reflection [9]. For an FMCG carton or bottle this is exactly why professional packshots look "correct" and casual ones look like they are leaning back. _(inference)_ Naming "perspective-control lens, verticals kept parallel, no keystone" is a strong prompt clause for any upright pack.

**Focus.** Two competing problems. Small products at close range have brutally shallow depth of field (a macro at f/5.6 can give roughly 2mm of depth), but stopping down to f/16 to fix it introduces **diffraction** which softens the whole frame [10, search-level]. The professional answer is **focus stacking**: shoot the lens's sharp aperture (f/5.6-f/8) across many frames at shifted focus points and merge [10]. The tilt movement on a tilt-shift is the analogue alternative · angling the plane of focus (the **Scheimpflug principle**) to lie along the subject rather than across it [9]. In a prompt this cashes out as "front-to-back sharpness, focus-stacked, no soft edges on the pack".

### Light

**The three-light grammar.** Key light (main source, classically about 45 degrees to one side and slightly above, angled down), fill (opposite side, roughly 50-70% of key, lifts the shadow side, often just a white bounce card or foam board rather than a second head), and rim / back light (behind the product aimed at its edge, creates separation, especially against a dark ground · keep it subtle or you get a halo) [3, 12].

**Modifiers, and what they actually do.** A **softbox** gives a broad even field that wraps and minimises harsh speculars [3, 12]. A **strip box** is the narrow version, used to lay a long directional highlight down the side of a bottle or can [3]. A **scrim** is a large translucent frame you blast a light through, turning a whole wall of the studio into one enormous soft source [12]. **Reflectors / bounce cards / V-flats** fill; silver boosts contrast, white is subtle [12]. **Flags** (black panels) block and shape; the same black panels function as **negative fill**, subtracting ambient to deepen one side and restore contrast [11]. A **light tent** gives a near-shadowless envelope for small hard-goods [12].

**Light the surface, not the object.** The single most useful idea for reflective packaging. Glossy surfaces obey the **law of reflection** · the angle light leaves equals the angle it arrived · so what you see on a shiny pack is not the light, it is a *picture of the light source* [4]. Therefore to get a soft-edged highlight on a gloss pack you must reflect a source that already has soft edges, which means building a **gradient** for the surface to see: a sheet of diffusion in front of a softbox with a gap between the two, giving a radial gradient from a spot or beauty dish, or a linear gradient from a strip box raked across the diffusion [4]. Matte surfaces do the opposite: they scatter, so highlights are broad, dim and soft-edged, and side light is what reveals their texture [4, 12].

**By material.** Glass wants **backlighting** to show transparency, or an **edge-lighting** setup with a light behind and slightly below aimed up, producing a bright background gradient that draws the glass edges as dark or bright lines [3]. Metal needs precise placement or you get distracting glare, and behaves like a mirror [12]. Matte cartons and paper want even diffused light plus one raking source for texture [12]. Liquids and splashes need very short flash duration (around 1/4000s) to freeze [4].

**Set-wide backgrounds.** A **seamless sweep** (paper curving from wall to table so there is no visible corner) is the cheap version; the built version is a **cyclorama / cyc wall / infinity cove / infinity curve**, a curved wall-to-floor backdrop with no edges at all [11]. The professional rule is to light the subject first and the background separately, holding roughly a one-stop difference between them, and to flag the background lights so their spill does not wrap onto the product as unwanted rim light and haze [11].

### Shadow

Shadow is the dial that most changes whether a generated FMCG scene reads as photographed. The real taxonomy [13, 5, 6]:

- **Cast shadow** · what the product throws onto the surface or background. It is the cue for light direction and it anchors the product [13].
- **Form shadow** · the shading on the object itself, on the faces turned away from the key. This is what reveals the shape of a bottle shoulder or a carton edge [13].
- **Contact shadow** · the tight, dark, almost-black core right where the object meets the surface. This is the ambient-occlusion cue; compositors call it out separately because it is the one most often missing or wrong, and without it a cut-out floats even when the cast shadow is perfect [7].
- **Reflected shadow** · light bouncing off nearby surfaces back onto the product, which is why a shadow on a glossy set is never neutral grey [13].
- **Hard vs soft shadow** · hard shadows come from small, distant, undiffused sources and have crisp edges; soft shadows come from large, close, diffused sources [5, 13]. Two levers control this and they pull in opposite directions: *diffusion* (fabric or scrim in front of the source softens) and *distance* (a source far away hardens the shadow; a source very close wraps around the object and barely casts at all) [5]. The sun is the reference hard source precisely because nothing is further away [5].
- **Gobo / cookie / cucoloris** · a shaped opaque panel placed between the light and the set to throw a patterned shadow. "Gobo" is short for *go between*; "cookie" is short for *cucoloris* [6]. Standard patterns are window frames and mullions, venetian blinds/shutters, doorways, fence rows, lace and mesh, and foliage · the **palm leaf** being the recognisable "summer" signature [6, 5]. Crisp gobo shadows need hard direct light placed far from the set [6, 5]. There is a commercial product category for exactly this (shadow boards / gobo shadow casters sold to product photographers), which confirms how mainstream the look is in packaged-goods imagery [6].
- **Floating / levitation shadow** · the product suspended (fishing line, a hidden acrylic block, or post work) with a soft detached shadow pooled below it rather than touching. The illusion depends on the shadow: even a floating object casts a diffuse shadow, and the shot fails if you omit it [14, search-level]. Low or eye-level viewpoints sell the float; overhead does not [14].
- **Reflection vs shadow.** On a glossy or acrylic surface the product does not cast a shadow so much as a **mirror reflection** with falloff. Getting these two confused is a classic tell · a gloss tabletop with a matte cast shadow reads as wrong. _(inference, grounded in the law-of-reflection framing in [4])_

### Surface and set

Art directors build product sets from a small kit of repeatable parts. **Risers** are short blocks, roughly 3 to 12 inches, used to lift one or more items above the table; **plinths** are the taller, more formal pedestals, roughly 12 to 36 inches, standing on the floor or a low table [15, search-level]. **Acrylic styling blocks** (clear polished bases, prisms, tiered stands) are the workhorse for flat lays and for lifting a pack off an uneven surface [15]. Stone slabs read as premium and are typically travertine (visible veining, organic pitted texture) or terrazzo (aggregate chips in a matrix), both of which have moved from architecture into product styling props [15]. Beyond that, the common ground planes are coloured paper sweeps, wood, slate, fabric, water and ice [4, 2].

Two things worth noting: the *material of the ground plane changes the shadow dial*, because a matte paper sweep takes a soft cast shadow, a polished stone takes a reflection plus a tight contact shadow, and water takes caustics. And the number of objects is really a set-styling decision · a single pack on a plinth is a hero packshot; three or more become a styled arrangement needing a focal hierarchy.

### Colour direction

The category conventions are unusually explicit:

- **Complementary** backdrop (opposite on the wheel · blue product on orange, red on green) maximises contrast and pop, and is the standard move when the job is to make one pack jump [2, 16].
- **Analogous** backdrop (adjacent hues) is the subtler, more harmonious, more editorial route [2, 16].
- **Monochromatic** · shades of one hue for a unified, sophisticated read [2].
- Contrast is not only hue. It can be run on saturation (vivid pack against a muted ground), brightness (light against dark), texture (shiny against matte) and lighting [2]. Photoroom cites that consumers are more likely to buy a strong product when packaging or background has high saturation contrast [2].
- Brand fit is a real constraint: backgrounds should sit with the site, the pack and the brand, and Photoroom's test is to mentally remove the product and ask whether the background still feels on-brand [2].
- Colours carry category signals: neutrals (grey, beige, off-white) for sophistication in fashion and home; black for drama and depth; blue for trust; green for nature, health, wealth; yellow for happiness; pastels for baby and beauty [16].

**Marketplace constraint.** Many marketplaces require at least one pure-white-background image, and white or light grey remains the default for the primary listing image because it removes distraction [16, 2]. I could not fetch Amazon's own image-requirements page (see Sources), so I am not citing a specific frame-fill percentage or pixel spec to Amazon. Treat "85% frame fill" and similar numbers as unverified until someone pulls the official page. The safe, sourced framing is: pure white is the marketplace packshot; coloured, textured and lifestyle grounds are for the site, the app tile and social [16, 2]. For a quick-commerce app tile specifically, the generated scene is closer to the second category than the first.

### Compositing a cut-out

This is the actual technical problem of this system: a transparent product PNG dropped into a generated world. The documented failure modes:

1. **Light direction mismatch.** The dominant amateur error: subject lit from the left, scene lit from the right. No blending fixes it, and shadows always point away from the light source, so a wrong cast shadow doubles the error [7].
2. **Light quality mismatch.** Soft/diffused subject in a hard/directional scene (or the reverse) [7].
3. **Colour temperature mismatch.** A subject shot around 5500K sitting in a scene built around 3000K reads as discordant; likewise cool-toned shadows in the plate against warm-toned shadows on the subject [7, 17].
4. **Grey shadows.** Real shadows are not grey · they carry the ambient colour of the scene. A generic neutral contact shadow gives a composite away instantly; the fix is to sample the shadow colour from the darker regions of the background rather than defaulting to black [17].
5. **Edge halo / fringing.** Boundary pixels carry the old background's colour, so a cut-out taken off white and dropped on a saturated ground glows at the edge [17]. Fine detail and semi-transparent material (mesh, foil, condensation, clear PET) are the worst cases.
6. **Gradient conflict.** Gradient backgrounds are unforgiving because the subject meets a different hue at different points along its edge, and a gradient implies a light direction that may fight the subject's actual light [17].
7. **Missing contact shadow.** Wherever the object touches the surface there must be a contact shadow, and this is the shadow most often omitted or done wrong [7 general principle; named explicitly in the cut-out compositing literature].

The most useful workflow inversion for this system: **match the background to the light already on the subject, rather than forcing the subject to fit a chosen background** [17]. That is exactly what a generate-around-the-PNG pipeline should do, and it is the argument for describing the desired scene light in terms that agree with whatever light the supplied pack was shot in. A practical check from the same source: blur the composite, invert it, desaturate it, and view at thumbnail size to catch mismatches [17].

## What separates a convincing result from a generic one

1. **The shadow is specified as a physical cause, not an adjective.** "Soft shadow" is a wish; "large diffused source close in, above and slightly left, soft-edged cast shadow falling right, with a tight dark contact shadow at the base" is a recipe [5, 13, 7]. Same for hard: hard shadows need *undiffused* light *far away* [5].
2. **The contact shadow is called out separately from the cast shadow.** Almost every fake-looking composite has a cast shadow and no contact shadow [7, 17].
3. **Shadows are tinted, not grey.** Sampling shadow colour from the scene's ambient rather than defaulting to black or grey is the named fix [17].
4. **The ground plane's material governs the shadow behaviour.** Gloss and acrylic want reflection with falloff; matte paper wants a diffuse cast shadow; stone wants both a soft cast and a tight occlusion line [4, 13]. A generic result treats every surface the same.
5. **The background is not evenly lit.** Real studio work holds about a stop between product and ground and lets the sweep fall off [11]. A flat even background is the tell of a generated packshot.
6. **The light on the scene agrees with the light already on the supplied PNG.** The compositing literature's own advice is to build the background around the subject's existing light, not the other way round [17]. This is the single highest-leverage instruction in a PNG-in, scene-out system.
7. **The lens is stated and long.** Wide-angle staging distorts packaging and reads as phone-grade; 85-100mm plus front-to-back sharpness is the category baseline [1, 4, 10].
8. **Verticals stay parallel on upright packs** [9].
9. **Colour is stated as a relationship, not a hex.** Because the product PNG varies, "background complementary to the pack's dominant hue, muted, so the pack holds saturation contrast" survives a product swap in a way that "orange background" does not [2, 16].
10. **Edges.** Fringing, halo and semi-transparent material (clear bottles, foil, condensation) are the known failure surface; a prompt that explicitly requires clean edges and correct treatment of transparent parts is doing real work [17].

## Model-side levers

What is actually documented for the target model family:

- **Prompt as a described scene, not a keyword list.** Google's own guidance structures a prompt around subject, composition, action, location and style [19]. The multimodal formula given for reference-image work is `[reference images] + [relationship instruction] + [new scenario]` · e.g. use image 1 as structure and image 2 as texture, then "place it in a sun-drenched, minimalist living room" [18].
- **Give each reference image an explicit role.** The guidance is to "clearly define the role of each" input when more than one image is supplied [19]. For this system that means naming the PNG as the product to be preserved, and everything else as scene direction.
- **State what must be preserved, explicitly.** The documented pattern is "ensure the silk texture and the model's facial features are preserved exactly" [search-level, Nano Banana guidance] and, in Google's own editing advice, being direct and specific about which elements stay unchanged while others are modified [19, 18]. The key principle stated outright: **be explicit about what stays the same** [18].
- **Photographic control language works.** Documented as responded-to: camera type, "shallow depth of field (f/1.8)", wide-angle, macro, "low-angle shot", "aerial view", "three-point softbox setup", "chiaroscuro lighting with harsh, high contrast", "golden hour backlighting creating long shadows", colour grading terms, and film-stock references [18, 19].
- **Specify materials as materials.** The documented improvement is replacing a category noun with a material noun · "navy blue tweed" instead of "suit jacket" [18]. Applied here: "honed travertine slab" instead of "stone surface".
- **Positive framing.** Describe what you want, not what you do not · "empty street", not "no cars" [18]. This matters for a shadow dial: "single soft cast shadow falling to the right" beats "no double shadows".
- **Open with an action verb** stating the primary operation [18].
- **Capacity.** Up to 14 reference object images per prompt; aspect ratio is natively controllable (1:1, 3:2, 16:9, 21:9 and others); output at 1K, 2K or 4K [18, 19].
- **Known failure modes**, from the prompting guidance: large edits such as background swap or major relighting can produce lighting mismatches, and the documented fix is to state the lighting direction to match or supply a lighting reference image [search-level Nano Banana guidance]; small objects and accessories are historically unreliable and want a micro-constraint plus a close-up reference; and an over-broad preserve list can fight the change list, causing the model to pass the input through unchanged [search-level Nano Banana guidance]. Google's own blog and cloud guide do not enumerate failure modes, so I am marking that last cluster as coming from secondary prompting guides rather than official documentation.

Nothing solid turned up on quick-commerce-specific (Zepto/Blinkit/Instamart) image standards, or on measured conversion effects of shadow style on app product tiles. I did not find it; do not invent it.

## Proposed clause upgrades

Each is phrased as prompt text to add or replace. Dial noted.

1. **[Camera angle] Replace bare angle names with viewpoint + angle + lens.** Add: `high three-quarter viewpoint, camera roughly 45 degrees above and 45 degrees around, shot on a 100mm macro at f/8`. Rationale: viewpoint and angle are two separate axes in the trade taxonomy and specifying both removes ambiguity, while the long lens suppresses the wide-angle distortion that marks amateur packshots. [8, 1, 4]
2. **[Camera angle] Add a verticals clause for all upright packs.** Add: `perspective-control framing, vertical edges of the pack kept parallel, no keystone convergence`. Rationale: converging verticals are the specific defect that makes a carton or bottle look like it is leaning back. [9]
3. **[Camera angle] Add a sharpness clause.** Add: `front-to-back sharpness across the pack, focus-stacked look, no soft falloff on the label`. Rationale: at product distances a single frame cannot hold the whole pack sharp, and stacking is the professional answer, so naming it gets the professional look. [10, 9]
4. **[Shadow · soft] Replace "soft shadow" with its physical cause.** Replace with: `large diffused source close above and slightly left, soft-edged cast shadow falling to the lower right, with a tight dark contact shadow where the pack meets the surface`. Rationale: softness is produced by source size, distance and diffusion, and the contact shadow is the cue most often missing from generated composites. [5, 13, 7]
5. **[Shadow · hard] Replace "hard shadow" with its physical cause.** Replace with: `single hard undiffused source placed far from the set, crisp-edged cast shadow with a defined core, high contrast`. Rationale: distance, not just lack of diffusion, is what hardens a shadow edge; the sun is the reference because nothing is further away. [5, 6]
6. **[Shadow · patterned] Use the trade name for the fashionable shadow.** Replace descriptions of leaf or blind shadows with: `gobo shadow cast across the surface, palm-frond cucoloris pattern, hard direct light, crisp edges`. Rationale: gobo/cucoloris/cookie are the real names for shaped shadow-casters and are heavily represented in commercial photography captions; the pattern plus the hard-light condition together produce the look. [6, 5]
7. **[Shadow · floating] Add a levitation clause.** Add: `product suspended just above the surface, no support visible, a detached soft shadow pooled directly beneath it, camera at or below product height`. Rationale: the illusion depends entirely on there still being a diffuse shadow, and low or eye-level viewpoints sell the float while overhead does not. [14]
8. **[Shadow] Tint the shadow.** Add to every shadow clause: `shadow colour drawn from the ambient of the scene, never neutral grey or black`. Rationale: grey contact shadows are named as the instant giveaway of a composite. [17]
9. **[Surface] Name the material precisely and let it drive the shadow.** Replace "stone surface" with `honed travertine slab with visible veining and organic pitting` or `terrazzo slab with fine aggregate chips`, and add `on gloss surfaces render a mirror reflection with falloff rather than a matte cast shadow`. Rationale: the model responds to material nouns over category nouns, and the law of reflection means gloss grounds behave differently from matte ones. [18, 15, 4]
10. **[Surface] Add real set furniture.** Add options: `product raised on a low riser`, `product on a formal plinth`, `product lifted on a clear acrylic styling block`. Rationale: risers, plinths and acrylic blocks are the actual staging kit with defined heights, and they give an elevation that reads as art-directed rather than accidental. [15]
11. **[Surface / background] Add background falloff.** Add: `seamless sweep curving from ground to backdrop with no visible corner, background held about one stop darker than the product, gentle falloff toward the edges`. Rationale: a flat, evenly-lit background is the studio tell that professionals avoid by lighting subject and ground separately. [11]
12. **[Colour theme] State colour as a relationship to the supplied product, not a fixed hue.** Replace fixed background colours with: `background hue complementary to the dominant colour of the supplied product, held desaturated so the pack keeps the strongest saturation in the frame` and offer an `analogous, low-contrast, editorial` alternative. Rationale: the supplied PNG changes every run, so a relational rule survives the swap, and saturation contrast is separately evidenced as a purchase-relevant lever. [2, 16]
13. **[Lighting, cross-dial] Add a material-aware light clause.** Add: `for gloss or metallic packaging, build a soft gradient for the surface to reflect: strip box raked down the side through a diffusion panel, producing a long soft-edged specular; for matte packaging, even diffusion plus one raking source to reveal texture`. Rationale: this is the "light the surface not the object" rule, the single most category-specific piece of craft for FMCG packaging. [4, 12, 3]
14. **[System-level, PNG preservation] Add an explicit preserve-and-match clause.** Add near the top of every prompt: `Use the supplied transparent PNG as the product. Preserve the product exactly: its geometry, label artwork, typography, colours and finish must be unchanged. Generate only the scene around it. Build the scene light to agree with the light already on the product: same direction, same hardness, same colour temperature. Render clean edges with no halo or fringe, and handle transparent or reflective parts of the pack as genuinely transparent.` Rationale: the documented multimodal formula is references plus a relationship instruction plus a new scenario, being explicit about what stays the same is the stated key principle, and the compositing literature's own advice is to build the background around the subject's existing light rather than the reverse. [18, 19, 17, 7]
15. **[System-level] Convert negative instructions to positive ones.** Replace "no double shadows / no extra products / no text" with `a single light source producing one cast shadow`, `exactly one product in frame`, `a clean unmarked surface`. Rationale: the guidance is explicit that describing what you want outperforms describing what you do not. [18]
