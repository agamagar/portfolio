# Schedule POV, model levers: research dossier
_Researched 2026-09-15. Sources listed at the bottom, numbered; cite by number inline._

Scope: the model side of generating first-person stills (own hands from the bottom edge, a switched-off phone or a task, Indian interiors and streets) on GPT Image 2 inside Figma and Nano Banana 2 via Weave. Tiers: VENDOR = OpenAI, Google, Ideogram, Midjourney, Black Forest Labs, Runway documentation; PRACTITIONER = a named person with a tested workflow, ideally with result images; COMMUNITY = forum threads, tag wikis, prompt collections. Anything not fetched is not a source. Where a page was reached through the r.jina.ai text proxy that is noted in the source list; the content is the page's own. Dashes inside quotations have been normalised to house punctuation; wording is otherwise verbatim.

Two dossiers already exist and are not repeated here: `models.md` (existing dossier: parameters, prose over keywords, clause order, negation policy per vendor, seeds, set consistency) and `schedule-images-phone-and-density.md` (existing dossier: phone as black glass, hand contact as a physical relation, 3 to 5 things in frame, POV and overhead flagged high-risk on video evidence). This one adds only what those lack: what the vendors say about viewpoint words, what "POV" actually does, the POV-specific failure modes, blur and ultra-wide, and the two target models.

Headline in three lines. (1) Neither OpenAI nor Google documents "POV", "first-person" or "from the subject's perspective" for their image models; OpenAI documents "top-down", Google documents "wide-angle", "low angle", "aerial view" and a GoPro hardware cue, and only Google's Imagen guide documents "fisheye" and "motion blur" [1][3][8][10][16]. (2) "POV" is ambiguous in the training vocabulary itself: the tag wiki that anime-trained models learned from defines it as a frame where a body part of the viewer is visible [40], Ideogram defines it as "from a character's visual perspective" [17], and Google's own Veo example of a "POV shot" is an over-the-shoulder frame with the character's head in it [22]. The one GPT Image 2 example found with "first-person POV" in the prompt rendered a person facing the camera [43]. So the word cannot carry the frame; the viewer's body part has to. (3) Version alert: OpenAI's prompting guide is now written for GPT Image 2.5 (Flare and Sunburst, launched September 2026) with GPT Image 2 kept as a reference model [3][5]; whether Figma's dropdown has moved is UNVERIFIED.

## What the vendors document

### OpenAI (GPT Image 2 and the new 2.5 guide)
- VENDOR. The gpt-image-2 cookbook guide documents viewpoint in one line: "Specify framing and viewpoint (close-up, wide, top-down), perspective/angle (eye-level, low-angle)" [1]. The same line appears in the gpt-image-1.5 guide [2]. So "top-down", "eye-level" and "low-angle" are documented vocabulary; "POV", "first-person", "bird's-eye", "wide-angle", "fisheye" and "motion blur" do not appear in either cookbook guide [1][2].
- VENDOR. The body and hands idiom is the strongest thing OpenAI gives us and it is repeated in the newest guide: "Describe body framing, relative scale, gaze, and interaction with objects. Instructions such as 'full body visible, feet included,' 'looking down at the open book,' or 'hands naturally gripping the handlebars' make the intended pose and action clearer" [3], with the cookbook version "describe scale, body framing, gaze, and object interactions" and "looking down at the open book, not at the camera" [1].
- VENDOR. Camera language is a cue, not physics. The current API prompting guide: "Treat camera specifications as cues for appearance, not a guarantee of exact physical simulation. For wide, cinematic, low-light, rainy, or neon scenes, specify scale, atmosphere, and color instead of relying on mood words alone" [3]. The cookbook example of a camera clause is "Shot like a 35mm film photograph, medium close-up at eye level, using a 50mm lens" [1][2]. INFERENCE: a lens number alone will not stretch the corners; the visible consequence has to be described.
- VENDOR. Limitations, verbatim, are four: "Latency", "Text Rendering", "Consistency" and "Composition Control: Despite improved instruction following, the model may have difficulty placing elements precisely in structured or layout-sensitive compositions" [4]. Hands, anatomy, camera angle and perspective are not mentioned anywhere in the guide [4], the model page [7], the Images 2.0 launch post [6] or the Images 2.5 launch post [5].
- VENDOR, version note. The API prompting guide now names "GPT Image 2.5 Flare" (small, speed) and "GPT Image 2.5 Sunburst" (base, quality), keeps "GPT Image 2" as the reference model, and gives shutdown dates for 1.5 (December 1, 2026) and 1 (October 23, 2026) [3]. The launch post claims 2.5 "follows editing instructions more reliably across multiple turns" and is "better at understanding complex visual instructions" [5]. Nothing in either page is about perspective. A practitioner review dated September 10, 2026 found that 2.5 "held character consistency across angles more reliably than Nano Banana 2 and Nano Banana Pro" on a four-angle grid from one reference, and notes OpenAI does not say which variant powers ChatGPT by default [36]. Figma's model list: UNVERIFIED (see existing dossier `models.md`, which found no Figma primary source naming an OpenAI model).

### Google (Gemini image, Nano Banana 2 and Pro)
- VENDOR. The API docs give camera as a fill-in slot: "Shot from a [camera angle] with a [lens type]." with the worked example "Shot from a low perspective with a wide-angle lens." and, for products, "The camera angle is a [angle type] to showcase [specific feature]." with "a slightly elevated 45-degree shot" [8]. The Interactions page repeats these and adds "Present a clear, 45° top-down isometric miniature 3D cartoon scene of London" [9].
- VENDOR. The Cloud prompting guide is the most explicit page either vendor has on camera control: "Control the camera: Use photographic and cinematic terms like 'low angle' and 'aerial view'." and "Choose your camera, lens, and focus: Use specific hardware and photographic terminology to control the depth, distortion, and perspective of your shot." Under hardware: "Dictate the exact camera type to change the visual DNA of the image. Ask for the shot to be taken on a GoPro for an immersive, distorted action feel, a Fujifilm camera for authentic color science, or a cheap disposable camera for a raw, nostalgic flash aesthetic." Under lens: "Force the perspective by explicitly requesting a 'low-angle shot with a shallow depth of field (f/1.8)'. If you need to show a vast scale, ask for a 'wide-angle lens'. For intricate details, specify a 'macro lens'" [10]. This is the only vendor sentence found that names an action camera as a prompt lever.
- VENDOR. The 2.5 Flash Image post: "Use photographic and cinematic language to control the composition. Terms like wide-angle shot, macro shot, low-angle perspective, 85mm portrait lens, and Dutch angle give you precise control over the final image" [12].
- VENDOR. The Nano Banana Pro tips page lists composition as "extreme close-up, wide shot, low angle shot, portrait" and camera as "A low-angle shot with a shallow depth of field (f/1.8)"; the phrase "POV shot" appears only in a storyboard caption ("establishing shot, medium shot, close-up, and POV shot"). The page does not contain fisheye, focal length, shutter speed, hands, motion blur, wide-angle, bird's-eye, overhead, first-person or top-down [11].
- VENDOR. The Pro launch post promises editing-time camera control: "Adjust camera angles, change the focus and apply sophisticated color grading, or even transform scene lighting" and "adjusting the depth of field or focal point" [13]. The DeepMind Pro page shows a focus-shift pair in which a "reaching hand blurred in the foreground" becomes "the sharp details of the hand", and a "high-angle, almost top-down photograph" [14]. The DeepMind Nano Banana 2 page uses "The camera is positioned at a low angle, looking upwards towards the subject", "wide-angle lens from an extremely low perspective" and "aerial shot" [15]. Both pages state the model "can still struggle with small faces, accurate spelling, and fine details" [14][15]; hands are not named.
- VENDOR, adjacent model. Google's Imagen prompt guide is the only Google page that documents the exact words this brief asked about: camera position "aerial, from below"; lens types "35mm, 50mm, fisheye, wide angle, macro"; camera settings "motion blur, soft focus, bokeh, portrait", with the example "photo of a city with skyscrapers from the inside of a car with motion blur"; a table pairing sports with "Fast shutter speed" and landscape with "Long exposure times"; and "an expansive mountain range, landscape wide angle 10mm" [16]. Imagen is not Nano Banana; transfer is INFERENCE, but Google's own prompting vocabulary is consistent across the two.
- VENDOR, video. Google's Veo 3.1 guide lists "POV shot" among camera movements and its worked example is "POV shot from behind the singer on stage, looking out at a large, cheering crowd... You can see the back of the singer's head and shoulders in the foreground" [22]. That is an over-the-shoulder frame. Google's own usage of "POV" therefore includes the character's head, which is the opposite of what this library wants.

### Other vendors that do define POV
- VENDOR, Ideogram. The only image vendor found with a definition: "Point of view (POV): from a character's visual perspective (also: first-person view)", listed separately from "Over-the-shoulder: behind a subject, viewing what they see". Also "Bird's-eye view: looking down from high above (also: aerial view, top-down perspective)", "Overhead view: directly above the subject", "Wide-angle view: expansive field of vision". And the one rule that matters for us: "When using angle or point-of-view terms in your prompts, make sure the other details you describe make sense from that same viewpoint" [17].
- VENDOR, Midjourney. Prompt Basics lists composition examples "portrait, headshot, closeup, birds-eye view", advises specific numbers over plurals ("three cats"), and on negation: "Describe what you do want instead of what you don't. If you mention a party with 'no cake,' a cake might still appear" [18]. The `--no` page shows "still life gouache painting without any fruit" and "please don't add fruit!" as ineffective and "--no fruit, apple, pear" as the working form [19].
- VENDOR, Black Forest Labs (FLUX.2). Camera is a structured block ("angle", "distance", "lens-mm", "f-number"), "Word order matters, FLUX.2 pays more attention to what comes first", and "FLUX.2 does not support negative prompts. Focus on describing what you want, not what you don't want" [20].
- VENDOR, Runway (video). "POV: The frame is what the character sees"; "Handheld: Subtle natural shake, slightly imperfect framing"; "Top-down / aerial: Camera points straight down from above" [21].

## How models read "POV" (evidence)

**The word means three different frames in the sources, which is the root of the drift.**
- Camera-as-eyes with the viewer's body in shot. COMMUNITY, but foundational: the Danbooru tag wiki, the labelling convention behind anime-trained diffusion checkpoints, defines pov as "An image in first-person point-of-view. Usually a body part of the viewer is visible, such as a penis or an arm, to indicate the image is in first-person view", routes frames with no viewer body part to "looking at viewer" instead, and has child tags pov_hands and pov_shadow [40]. VENDOR: Ideogram, "from a character's visual perspective" [17]. VENDOR video: Runway, "The frame is what the character sees" [21]. PRACTITIONER video: "the camera is the character's eyes" and name "what body parts are visible (hands, feet)" [30].
- Over-the-shoulder. VENDOR: Google's Veo POV example has "the back of the singer's head and shoulders in the foreground" [22]. Ideogram keeps over-the-shoulder as a separate term [17].
- Someone looking at the viewer. COMMUNITY with a result image: in a GPT Image 2 prompt collection, "intimate first-person low-angle POV shot from below" produced, per the displayed result, a young woman in basketball attire shot from below with harsh on-camera flash, i.e. a portrait of another person facing the lens, no viewer hands [43]. That is exactly the "face in a first-person shot" failure this brief asked about, on the target model.

**What put hands in frame in the fetched examples was never the word POV; it was a body-part or genre clause.**
- GPT Image 2, COMMUNITY with result image: "A first-person gameplay screenshot of a cozy lakeside stone cottage in a lush block-built survival world at golden hour" rendered with the player's hand visible in the lower right [43]; the same prompt appears in a practitioner guide as a working example [35]. INFERENCE: "gameplay screenshot" carries a hands-in-frame convention from FPS games; "first-person" alone did not carry it in the portrait example above.
- Nano Banana, PRACTITIONER with result image: an arm in frame was obtained by describing the arm's action, "extending their arm towards the lens as if holding the camera", inside an "extreme ultra-wide 'fisheye' lens (8mm)" selfie prompt [23]. The author adds: "it does take a few tries to get a realistic character reference, and you won't be able to one-shot it every time" [23].
- Nano Banana Pro, PRACTITIONER with images: "POV shot, first-person perspective, immersive realism" is shown with a result, but the page does not state whether the viewer's hands are in it, and the author warns "It's not necessary that you will get 100% similar results as shown in this tutorial but mostly similar" [37]. UNVERIFIED for hands.
- Midjourney, PRACTITIONER: the "First-person view --v 6.1" style page describes environments "with a focus on foreground elements and wide-angle views"; hands are not mentioned [29]. Stable Diffusion 1.x, COMMUNITY: a dedicated "First Person View" embedding (trigger `fpv`) exists because the bare tag was unreliable, and its author says "Sometimes it's easy to achieve a look but many times you'll have to be accurate with the wording (prompts)!!" [41].
- Prompt syntax that names the anchor, PRACTITIONER: "point-of-view shot from [character], foreground hands or object establish perspective" [34]. COMMUNITY: "Create an image of me [insert any adventure here], but make it from my POV. Remember to keep the image POV", with the claim that the repeated instruction is a guardrail against drift back to third person; images are shown, but there is no controlled with-and-without comparison [42].

**Does "POV" reliably produce camera-as-eyes on the two target models?** Nothing solid found: no fetched source runs a controlled test on GPT Image 2 or Nano Banana 2. The video evidence in the existing phone dossier ("Text prompts alone rarely produce a correct POV") stands, and the video practitioner's ladder puts a phone-shot mock reference as "the single most reliable POV unblock" [30]. Phrasings the brief asked about, with status: "shot from the wearer's perspective" (nothing solid found), "the camera is her eyes" (video only [30][21]), "GoPro chest mount" (Google documents "GoPro" as a hardware cue for "an immersive, distorted action feel" [10]; "chest mount" as a phrase: nothing solid found), "first person view" (Ideogram alias [17]; SD needed an embedding [41]), "hands in frame" (the definitional requirement in [40], the working lever in [23][43][34]).

## Failure modes and documented counters

1. **A face appears in the first-person shot.** Evidence: the GPT Image 2 "first-person POV shot from below" portrait [43]; the tag semantics that route a face-to-camera frame to "looking at viewer", not pov [40]; Google's own POV example containing the character's head [22]. Counter with evidence: define the frame by the viewer's body part, since that is literally what makes an image pov in the labelling convention [40] and what practitioners say establishes the perspective [34][30]; then obey Ideogram's rule and describe nothing that could not be seen from your own eyes (no expression, no face, no back of the head) [17]. OpenAI's gaze idiom cannot be used as written because there is no face to point away; its structural equivalent is a body-framing clause ("only my forearms and hands are in the picture") [1][3]. Negation ("no face") is documented to work on OpenAI generally (existing dossier `models.md`) but a community thread shows five stacked "no X" clauses failing to suppress noise on the 2.0 generator [44]; Google prefers positive phrasing (existing dossier). Nothing solid found on "no face" wording specifically.

2. **Arm from the wrong edge, or a third arm.** Evidence for POV specifically: nothing solid found. General hand evidence: keep "hands to one subject at a time", "two people gripping the same object" compounds errors, "Wrong thumb position: The opposable thumb location is difficult when the hand is at non-standard angles", and "prompt the specific hand (left/right) and its orientation" [32]; the Stable Diffusion 1.x era answer was a negative list including "(((extra arms)))" plus inpainting [39], which does not transfer to models without a negative field (existing dossier). Counter, INFERENCE from the count rule in Midjourney's docs ("three cats" not "cats") [18] and the left/right rule [32]: state the count and the edge as a positive fact, "both forearms enter from the bottom edge of the frame", and name which hand does what.

3. **Wrong finger counts when two hands are in frame.** Evidence: the hard cases named by practitioners are "multiple people with overlapping or interlocking hands, a hand wrapped around a complex object, a hand rendered small in a wide or crowded shot" [31] and "interlaced fingers" or "pointing" poses [32]. OpenAI lists no hand limitation at all [4], and a practitioner reads that as basic hands being mostly solved on flagship models, which is a claim without a shown test [31]. Counter with evidence: "Describe what the hand or face is doing, not that it exists. 'A relaxed handshake, palms fully visible, four fingers and a thumb on each hand'" [31]; "if you don't need hands in the image, compose to exclude them" [32]; OpenAI's "hands naturally gripping the handlebars" [1][3]. For this library: one hand does the task, the other rests flat or leaves the frame, which is the existing phone dossier's clause 3 with two more sources behind it.

4. **The phone rendered from the back, or mirrored.** Nothing solid found. Searches returned stock photo pages and one product-insights article that returned 404 when fetched. The only related vendor fact is OpenAI's object-interaction idiom [1][3]. Counter is INFERENCE only: state the phone's orientation as a physical relation to the viewer ("the dark glass screen faces me, the camera bump is hidden against my palm"), the same move the existing phone dossier made for the grip. UNVERIFIED, first-run test.

5. **Horizon and tilt reading as over-the-shoulder instead of own eyes.** Evidence: the vendors themselves blur the two, Google's POV example is over-the-shoulder [22] and Ideogram lists over-the-shoulder as a neighbouring term [17]; a video practitioner names "floating camera" and "third-person drift" as the two ways POV fails [30]. Counter with evidence: name the camera position as a body posture rather than a shot type, which is Runway's and invideo's "the frame is what the character sees" / "the camera is the character's eyes" [21][30] and Google's "The camera is positioned at a low angle, looking upwards" pattern with the body substituted for the tripod [15]; and keep every described detail visible from that posture [17]. Stills evidence on the two targets: nothing solid found.

6. **Overhead frames flipping to eye level.** Evidence for stills: numeric camera notation is ignored on OpenAI's image models ("Looks like 30° yaw and -30° yaw are ignored"), descriptive spatial language worked better, and one user found the view did not change inside the same chat but did in a fresh one [45]; the video evidence for drift on "extreme verticals" is in the existing phone dossier. Working phrasings with result images: "Shot from directly above, 50mm lens" for a flat lay on GPT Image 2 [43]; "bird's-eye view, camera directly above the subject, geometric composition" on Nano Banana Pro [37]. Counter: say "directly above" or "perpendicular to the table", because "bird's-eye" is defined by Ideogram as "from high above (also: aerial view)" [17] and Google pairs it with "aerial" [10][15], so it can read as a drone shot rather than a tabletop; and use a reference image where the angle is load-bearing [30][45].

7. **Negative phrasing: what works and what backfires.** Documented to fail: "no cake" and "without any fruit" on Midjourney [18][19]; not supported on FLUX.2 [20]. The stronger claim that negatives "can backfire and add exactly what you're trying to avoid" is asserted for FLUX.2 in a third-party guide with no test [46]; it is not in BFL's own page [20]. On OpenAI, negation is documented usable (existing dossier) but not a guarantee [44]. On Google, positive phrasing is the documented preference (existing dossier). Rule for this library: describe the wanted state ("the only person in the picture is me, seen as two forearms"); add a short exclusion only on the GPT Image register, and only for the specific default that keeps going wrong.

## Motion blur and ultra-wide

### Motion blur
- VENDOR. Google's Imagen guide lists "motion blur" as a camera setting and shows it in a moving-vehicle frame, "photo of a city with skyscrapers from the inside of a car with motion blur"; it pairs freezing with "Fast shutter speed" and smoothing with "Long exposure times" [16]. Neither Gemini image page nor any OpenAI page documents motion blur [1][3][8][10][11]. OpenAI's stance on all camera numbers: "cues for appearance, not a guarantee of exact physical simulation" [3].
- PRACTITIONER, Midjourney, seed-controlled with images. The word "shutter" is a blur trigger regardless of the number: "In general, Midjourney doesn't handle shutter speeds well. Mention of the word shutter or shutter speed seems to cause motion blur, no matter the specified shutter speed" [24]. A second fixed-seed test found shutter values (1/4000s, 1/4s), ISO, f-stops, focal-length numbers and camera brand names had no consistent effect, while effect words did: "motion blur", "long exposure", "freeze frame", "grainy footage", "bokeh", plus iconic cameras and named film stocks [25]. Benchmarks: "Long exposure photography" scored 8.0 with one static failure ("decaying peonies stayed still") [27]; the "Motion blur" style at v6.1 is described as "dreamy and surreal aesthetic, characterized by dynamic brushwork and blended textures", i.e. it can go painterly rather than photographic [28].
- Walking POV blur specifically: nothing solid found for stills on any model. Video vendor vocabulary is the nearest: "Handheld: Subtle natural shake, slightly imperfect framing" [21]. INFERENCE for the clause: name the physical cause (walking, handheld phone, dim light, slow shutter) and name what must stay readable (the forearm and the phone), since the evidence says effect words register and numbers do not [24][25], and "long exposure" carries a photographic register that "motion blur" alone may not [27][28]. UNVERIFIED on GPT Image 2 and Nano Banana 2.

### Ultra-wide and fisheye
- VENDOR. Google: "wide-angle lens" to "show a vast scale" [10]; "Shot from a low perspective with a wide-angle lens" [8]; "wide-angle lens from an extremely low perspective" [15]; GoPro "for an immersive, distorted action feel" [10]; Imagen lists "fisheye, wide angle" and "landscape wide angle 10mm" [16]. OpenAI: nothing on wide or fisheye; numbers are cues [3].
- PRACTITIONER, Nano Banana, with result images. Both extremes worked when the lens was named and the visible effect described: "An extreme low-angle, wide-angle 'fisheye' shot... The camera is placed virtually on the ground, making their legs and shoes appear massive and dominant... Shot on a 14mm ultra-wide lens", and "captured through an extreme ultra-wide 'fisheye' lens (8mm), creating a fun, distorted circular perspective with heavy vignetting at the corners" [23].
- PRACTITIONER, Midjourney. "Fisheye lens works great in Midjourney prompts!" at 8.5/9 on v4, with one image that "only went halfway" [26]; focal-length numbers alone had no predictable effect at fixed seed [25].
- PRACTITIONER, no test shown. A prompt guide claims "20 to 24mm reads as a wide interior shot without curving" and that "no barrel distortion, straight lines at the edges of frame" settles residual bowing; it names no model and shows no images [33]. Treat as untested.
- Rule with evidence: name the lens and describe the consequence in the same breath (corners stretch, near hands loom large, straight lines bow), because Google tells you to use hardware terms to "control the depth, distortion, and perspective" [10] and OpenAI tells you the number is only a cue [3]; the shown Nano Banana successes did exactly this [23].

## Proposed clause upgrades

Ordered per the existing `models.md` rule: non-negotiables first, scene next, register and constraints last. Each is written to survive the observed 1,000-character Figma field. All are proposals with sources behind the levers, not tested prompts; see the last section.

1. **Frame anchor, first clause, never dropped.** "A real photograph seen through my own eyes: the camera is my eyes, and my own two forearms enter from the bottom edge of the frame. I am the only person in the picture." Rationale: the viewer's body part is what defines a first-person frame [40][34][30]; "the camera is my eyes" names the camera position as a body part [21][30]; the count is explicit [18]; positive framing satisfies Google and needs no negation on Gemini (existing dossier). Do not open with "POV shot" or "first-person POV": the one GPT Image 2 example with that wording produced a portrait [43], and Google's own POV example is over-the-shoulder [22].

2. **Posture as camera position (one of five variants).** Table: "I am seated, looking down at the table in front of me." Lying: "I am lying on my back; the phone is held above my face and the ceiling is behind it." Straight down: "I am standing and looking straight down at the phone; the floor is far below it." Walking: "I am walking; the street is ahead of me and my arm swings into the bottom of the frame." Overhead: "shot from directly above, camera perpendicular to the table, my forearms entering from the bottom edge." Rationale: OpenAI's gaze and body-framing idiom [1][3]; Google's "camera is positioned at ... looking ..." pattern [15]; "directly above" phrasings have shown results where "bird's-eye" is defined as aerial [43][37][17]. Ideogram's consistency rule: describe nothing (face, expression, back of head) that this posture could not see [17].

3. **One hand does the task, the other is accounted for.** "My right hand [chops the onion / holds the tape against the gift paper]; my left hand rests flat on the board, fingers relaxed." Rationale: describe what the hand is doing [31]; keep hands to one job and name left/right [32]; OpenAI's "hands naturally gripping" [1][3]. The existing phone dossier's clause 3 (hide or crop the second hand) remains the fallback.

4. **Phone as a physical relation to me.** "In my right hand, an iPhone 16 Pro Max, matte black, held upright; its dark glass screen faces me and reflects the room; my thumb rests on the screen edge." Rationale: the existing phone dossier's black-glass clause, extended with the orientation stated as a relation [1][3]. The orientation words are INFERENCE and untested.

5. **Scene, three to five named things.** "[Indian home kitchen / open-plan office desk / auto-rickshaw back seat / local train window / street pavement], [one light source], [one or two set-specific objects]." Rationale: existing phone dossier density rule; Google's one subject, one action, one location structure (existing dossier).

6. **Lens and motion register, only where the frame calls for it.** Walking: "handheld mid-stride, slow shutter in dim light, long-exposure smear across the street, while my forearm and the phone stay readable." Overhead and straight-down: "shot on a GoPro-like 14mm ultra-wide, the corners of the frame stretch and my near hand looms large." Rationale: effect words register and numbers do not [24][25]; "long exposure" has the strongest photographic evidence [27]; GoPro and wide-angle are Google's documented levers [10][8]; naming the visible consequence is what worked on Nano Banana [23] and what OpenAI's "cues for appearance" rule implies [3].

7. **Constraints last, register-specific.** GPT Image register: "No face, no shoulders, no second person; real candid photograph, no studio polish." Nano Banana register: restate positively, "the only person is me, seen as two forearms and hands; a candid phone snapshot." Rationale: OpenAI negation documented usable, not guaranteed [44]; Google positive phrasing; Midjourney and FLUX.2 vendor pages show why bare "no X" is unsafe as a general habit [18][19][20].

8. **Reference image when the angle is load-bearing.** For the lying-on-back, straight-down and true-overhead frames, attach a phone-shot mock of the frame and name its role ("Image 1: camera angle and hand placement reference"). Rationale: the video practitioner calls a phone mock "the single most reliable POV unblock" [30]; on OpenAI's models numeric angle notation was ignored and descriptive plus reference approaches worked [45]; role-naming is the cross-vendor rule (existing dossier).

Worked compact example for the table frame (about 760 characters, untested): "A real photograph seen through my own eyes: the camera is my eyes, and my own two forearms enter from the bottom edge of the frame. I am the only person in the picture, seated, looking down at a wooden kitchen table. My right hand holds an iPhone 16 Pro Max, matte black, upright; its dark glass screen faces me and reflects the window; my thumb rests on the screen edge. My left hand rests flat on the table beside a steel tumbler of chai. Indian home kitchen, morning window light from the left, a folded newspaper at the far edge. Candid phone snapshot, slight handheld softness. No face, no shoulders, no second person, no studio polish."

## Unverified, test in the first run

- Whether "POV" or "first-person" alone yields a camera-as-eyes frame on GPT Image 2 or Nano Banana 2: no controlled test exists in any fetched source. The one GPT Image 2 example rendered a portrait [43]. Run A/B: with and without the body-part anchor, same seedless prompt, five draws each.
- Whether "the camera is my eyes" transfers from video models [21][30] to stills. Test against "seen through my own eyes" and against no camera sentence.
- Whether "both forearms enter from the bottom edge of the frame" controls the edge, and whether it lowers the third-arm rate. Nothing solid found either way.
- Phone orientation words ("its dark glass screen faces me", "camera bump hidden against my palm"): nothing solid found; pure inference.
- The lying-on-back frame: no fetched source shows a first-person lying-down result with hands (the one community example found returned HTTP 403). Highest-risk frame; give it the reference image from the start.
- The overhead flip rate on stills for these two models: only video evidence exists (existing dossier) plus the OpenAI-community note that view changes sometimes only took effect in a fresh chat [45]. Test "shot from directly above" against "bird's-eye view" on both models.
- Walking blur phrasing on GPT Image 2 and Nano Banana 2: all blur evidence is Midjourney [24][25][27][28]. Test "long exposure" versus "motion blur" versus "slow shutter", and check whether "motion blur" goes painterly.
- Ultra-wide on GPT Image 2: OpenAI says camera numbers are cues [3]; the shown successes are Nano Banana [23]. Test "14mm ultra-wide, corners stretch" against "wide-angle lens" alone.
- "GoPro" as a hardware cue [10]: whether it drags in a GoPro-style colour and fisheye look that fights the candid-phone register, and whether it adds a visible camera or mount (the existing phone dossier records "close to the camera" summoning a camera on an older model).
- Whether the "no face, no shoulders" exclusion on the GPT Image register helps, does nothing, or backfires; and whether the positive-only Nano Banana register leaks a face without it.
- Which model Figma's dropdown actually runs now: OpenAI's guide is written for GPT Image 2.5 Flare and Sunburst [3][5]; a reviewer believes Flare is the ChatGPT default [36]. The 1,000-character field cap remains unsourced (existing dossier).
- Whether Nano Banana Pro's documented "adjust camera angles" edit [13] can convert a third-person result into a first-person one, or only nudges mild angles (the one practitioner test found only mild side-angle changes stayed consistent [38]).
- The "keep the image POV" repetition guardrail [42]: community claim with images but no with-and-without comparison.
