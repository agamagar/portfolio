Where an original quote contained a long dash it has been trimmed or paraphrased (house rule: no dash characters in this file). INFERENCE marks a physical reading that follows from a cited fact but is not itself stated by the source. UNVERIFIED marks a claim that surfaced only in a search snippet whose page could not be fetched.

## What the medium actually is

Indian candid lifestyle stills are photographs made under light that the subject did not arrange. The people who describe that light most precisely are the cinematographers who have to reproduce it, and the pattern across their interviews is consistent: the realist Indian look is built from the light sources that are already in the room, named as objects, not as moods.

Three practitioner statements set the register.

Anil Mehta on Badlapur (2015), asked what the lighting set-up was: "Two tube lights and a bulb. And that's not a joke." The actual kit was "a couple of KINOS and a few 650W/300W Fresnels, a 2K was thrown in as safety net" plus "we bought a commercial sodium vapour fixture and put it in the Tempo." On the apartment set: "The paint on the walls, the view from the windows, the props, the drapes, the naked tube light, determined the 'look'." On the scene he is proudest of: "I switched on one tube light and a bulb and sat there." [1]

Ravi K. Chandran, on how Indian rooms actually read: "Day interiors generally remain semi-darker with ambient sources… whereas at night, a single tube light source becomes brighter." And on the sun: "In India, the sun's arc rises perpendicularly after 8 a.m., dramatically changing the light quality, unlike Europe, where the sun's arc is ideal for shooting all day." He also notes the era shift in domestic bulbs: "earlier we had warm bulbs, but now LED lights have changed the principle and look." [3]

Michal Sobocinski PSC on The Disciple (Mumbai, 2020): "Chaitanya wasn't looking for big shafts of light coming through windows, or hazing up the interiors with atmosphere ... we had SkyPanels rigged up in every single room to make it look as if the lighting was just coming from the sources that are visible in the frame." He also warns the outsider's eye: "India has great sunsets, and it's a country filled with beautiful colors and women wearing beautiful saris. Those types of things could drive your attention to visual elements that are not as important." [6]

So the medium, physically, is: a small number of hard, cheap, differently coloured sources (a bare tube high on a wall, a bulb, a street lamp through a grill, a phone), a sun that goes overhead early and stays there, and a seasonal atmosphere (dust, monsoon overcast, winter fog) that decides whether that sun is hard or diffuse. The two things a prompt must therefore carry are the visible source (what it is, where it sits, what colour it throws) and the sky condition that governs the daylight.

The day interior corollary from Chandran [3] matters for stills: an Indian daytime room is typically darker than the street outside, lit by ambient bounce from a window or door, not by fixtures; the fixtures come on at night and then dominate.

## Time of day and weather

### Golden hour, and how short it is
Golden hour is the band with "the sun ... between the horizon and about 6° above it" [28]. Its length depends on latitude: at Los Angeles the sun reaches 10 to 12 degrees an hour after sunrise, and "For a location closer to the Equator, the same altitude is reached in less than an hour" [20]. Colour: "around 2,000 K shortly after sunrise or before sunset", "around 3,500 K during 'golden hour'", "around 5,500 K at midday"; the light is "redder and softer than when the sun is higher in the sky", with "longer shadows" and "shadows are less dark" [20]. The same geometry governs twilight: "at the equator, civil twilight can last as little as 24 minutes" against 33 to 48 minutes at Greenwich [21]. Mumbai is at 19 N and Chennai at 13 N; INFERENCE: the Indian golden hour is closer to 30 minutes than to 60, and the sky goes from gold to dark in one continuous slide rather than lingering. Practitioner corroboration: Chandran's "the sun's arc rises perpendicularly after 8 a.m." [3]; Santosh Sivan "prefers to shoot during the golden hour and restricts the use of kino fills in his shots" [29]; Ravi Varman: "One evening, I saw the light and asked Kamal Haasan to do the shot before it went" [7b].

### Harsh noon sun
Chandran's statement is the physical one: after about 8 a.m. the Indian sun is effectively overhead, and it stays there [3]. Ranabir Das on the Ratnagiri coast sequence in All We Imagine as Light: "that very top sun is like, it's not very pleasant. So that feeling, we wanted to kind of get", and the colourist "would bleach out some of the background when it was a very wide shot" so the sunlight would not "stick to that cliche of a pretty place" [11]. Amit Roy: the first-half hero "is shot in Mumbai's harsh sunlight" [10]. INFERENCE from the overhead angle: shadows fall straight down (eye sockets, under the nose, under the chin, under the cap peak), horizontal surfaces blow out while vertical faces stay in shade, and the ground bounces a lot of light back up, which is why interiors near an open door are lit from below the window line.

### Monsoon overcast
Pankaj Kumar on Tumbbad: "The film had to be moody and gloomy. There was to be no sunlight ... without a clear demarcation between day and night." "Although we shot during the monsoon to get the light, we didn't get much actual rain that year ... all I wanted was a cloudy day and no sunlight. Sometimes, we had to wait for hours just to get cloud cover." Palette: "blue and cold grey, splashed with striking red and gold" [8]. Kapadia and Das on All We Imagine as Light: "the first half is very cloudy, and the second half is very bright sun. The color palette also shifts in the process" [11]; the film's night look has been described as the "blueness of night with blurred yellow lights in distant high-rise windows" (UNVERIFIED: review snippet, page returned 403). Overcast daylight sits at 6500 K [19]. Kapoor & Sons was lit "mostly soft light, bounced or diffused to provide a natural feel that was real to the cloudy town of Coonoor" (UNVERIFIED: snippet, page 403). INFERENCE: monsoon light is a top-down skylight at 6500 K with no direction, wet surfaces act as mirrors that double every practical, and blue tarpaulins on roofs (a Mumbai monsoon fixture noted in the All We Imagine coverage) add a blue bounce into lanes.

### Pre-monsoon dust haze
NASA Earth Observatory: "Through April, May, and June, as monsoon conditions build, the air over the Ganges River plain grows thick with dust, smoke, and haze"; "westerly winds rush in to fill the void, bringing dust-laden air from the deserts of southwest Asia and the Arabian Peninsula"; the haze is "a combination of dust, smoke from fires, and urban smog" with dust the bulk of it; "air quality over India is at its worst at this time of year" [25]. INFERENCE for the image: the sky is white to pale ochre rather than blue, the sun is a soft disc you can look at, shadows lose their edge even at noon, and distance goes milky within a few hundred metres.

### Winter fog in Delhi and the Indo-Gangetic plain
The IMD's MAUSAM paper: "Fog is localized phenomenon where horizontal visibility is reduced to less than 1000 m"; "In India, radiation and advection fog are most common which occur mostly over north India in winter"; "Radiation fog usually occurs at late night when the earth radiates long wave terrestrial radiation"; it "gets dissipated after sunrise"; favourable conditions are "clear or light clouded sky" and "calm or light surface winds"; fog is present "over Indo-Gangetic plains on an average of 7 to 10 days during December and more than 8 days during January"; typical fog-hour conditions are wind 0.6 m/s and air temperature 9 to 15 C. The dense-fog code means "objects not visible at 50 m" by day [26]. INFERENCE: dawn images in a north-Indian winter are a monochrome grey with a single orange sodium or white LED pole lamp as the only colour, every source gets a visible glow halo, and contrast collapses beyond 50 to 200 m.

### Blue hour
Civil twilight is the sun "between the horizon and 6° below the horizon" and lasts about 24 minutes at the equator [21]. INFERENCE: the Indian blue hour is a 20 to 25 minute window in which a 6500 K to deeper-blue sky is balanced against 2000 K sodium or 4000 to 6500 K LED street lamps and the 6500 K tubes now switching on in windows. Sudeep Chatterjee names the old way of faking it: "for twilight scenes, we used HMIs for our bounce and we had to add blue gels to all the lights to adjust for the time of day" [13].

### Power cut and inverter light
R. Rathnavelu on a power-cut sequence: "The only source of light were the bike's headlight and a few emergency lights" [14]. Fonseca on the Chamkila house: "there was no electricity, the whole place was dark with only candles and oil lamps available" [12]. Avinash Arun and Neeraj Ghaywan scouted Masaan in Varanasi during cyclone Phailin "with lights gone off everywhere" (UNVERIFIED: search snippet of an interview whose page failed on certificate). Nothing solid found on the colour or quality of Indian inverter-run tube lights; INFERENCE only: an inverter runs one tube or one LED per room, so the house drops to a single source per room and the street outside goes dark, which pushes the phone screen and the diya to the top of the hierarchy.

## Proposed clause phrasings

Each clause is written to be dropped into a lifestyle prompt as a light clause. Rationale and sources follow each.

1. Night interior, tube light dominant
"Lit by one bare fluorescent tube light mounted high on the wall, cool white with a faint green cast left uncorrected, the brightest thing in the room; the rest of the room falls off into shadow."
Rationale: Chandran's night rule (single tube becomes brighter) [3], Mehta's naked tube [1], fluorescent colour and skin behaviour [15][17]. The negative ("uncorrected") is needed because models default to neutral white.

2. Night interior, mixed sources
"Mixed household lighting: a cool fluorescent tube on the wall, a warm 60 W bulb hanging from the ceiling, and a phone screen under the face, each a different colour, none corrected to the other."
Rationale: Mehta's "two tube lights and a bulb" kit [1], tungsten yellow [5][7], screen at 6500 K plus [19]. Naming three colours in one sentence is what produces the signature look.

3. Day interior
"Daytime room, semi-dark, lit only by ambient light bouncing in through the open door and window; no fixtures on; the street outside several stops brighter than the room."
Rationale: Chandran's day-interior rule [3]; Sobocinski's no-shafts, sources-in-frame approach [6].

4. Street at night, older road or small town
"Orange high-pressure sodium street lamp on a pole overhead, about 2000 K, skin and clothes rendered in near-monochrome amber with no blue anywhere; long hard shadows down the lane."
Rationale: HPS colour and CRI [18] (CCT figure UNVERIFIED), "the amber of the night" [5]. Use for period or small-town scenes.

5. Street at night, present-day city
"Cool white LED street lighting on poles, sharp pool of light under each pole and dark between them, a little blue in the shadows; no orange sodium."
Rationale: SLNP scale [22], Delhi and Bengaluru replacement status [23][24]. The explicit "no orange sodium" is the fact the library got wrong before.

6. Street lamp through a grill
"A street lamp outside throws the window-grill pattern across the far wall of the dark room, cool white (or orange sodium for an older street), the only light in the room."
Rationale: pole height and window geometry (INFERENCE), colour from [18][22]. The grill is the Indian-specific detail that anchors the source.

7. Zero-watt bulb bedroom
"Bedroom lit by a single zero-watt night bulb low on the wall, deep orange, very dim, lighting the face from the side at pillow height."
Rationale: wattage, use and position [27]; colour INFERENCE from low filament temperature [19].

8. Phone screen
"Face lit from below by a phone screen, cool blue-white at about 6500 K against the warm orange bulb behind, a rectangular catchlight in each eye."
Rationale: screen white point [19]; contrast against 2400 K tungsten [19].

9. Diwali diyas and fairy lights
"Lit by clay diyas along the sill at about 1850 K, deep orange and flickering, throwing moving shadows up the face, with strings of warm fairy lights draped on the balcony behind."
Rationale: candle-flame colour [19], Kumar on lamp light moving across faces [8], Fonseca on candle-and-oil-lamp darkness [12]. Fairy-light colour UNVERIFIED.

10. Golden hour
"Late golden hour, sun a few degrees above the horizon at about 3000 K, long soft shadows, the window of warm light short and already going."
Rationale: golden hour Kelvin and shadow description [20], short twilight at low latitude [21], Chandran on the perpendicular sun [3]. "Short and already going" is a textual cue for the compressed Indian window.

11. Harsh noon
"Overhead sun near noon, shadows falling straight down, dark eye sockets and a hard shadow under the chin, sunlit ground bouncing light back up, colour bleached in the wide shot."
Rationale: perpendicular sun [3], "very top sun ... not very pleasant" and bleached backgrounds [11], Mumbai harsh sunlight [10].

12. Monsoon overcast
"Monsoon overcast, full cloud cover and no sun, flat 6500 K skylight with no shadows, wet street and wet leaves mirroring every light, blue tarpaulins on the roofs."
Rationale: Kumar's "cloudy day and no sunlight" [8], overcast at 6500 K [19], first-half-cloudy palette in All We Imagine [11]. Tarps: coverage of the film (secondary).

13. Pre-monsoon haze
"May afternoon in north India, dust haze, white sky with no blue, the sun a soft disc, shadows without edges, distance going milky within a few hundred metres."
Rationale: NASA on April to June dust and smoke over the Ganges plain [25]; visual rendering is INFERENCE.

14. Delhi winter fog
"Delhi winter dawn, dense radiation fog, visibility under 50 metres, monochrome grey with a halo around each street lamp, no sun."
Rationale: IMD fog definitions, timing and dense-fog code [26]; halo and monochrome are INFERENCE.

15. Blue hour
"Blue hour, twenty minutes after sunset, deep blue sky balanced against the first orange sodium and cool LED street lamps and the tube lights switching on in windows."
Rationale: civil twilight length at low latitude [21], sky versus lamp contrast [13][18][22].

16. Power cut
"Power cut, no mains light, the room lit by a single inverter tube light and a phone screen, the street outside dark."
Rationale: Rathnavelu's emergency-light sequence [14], Fonseca's candle-only house [12]; inverter behaviour is INFERENCE and needs a source before it ships as fact.

17. Ceiling fan
"A ceiling fan turning under a bare ceiling bulb, its blade shadows sweeping across the wall and the face."
Rationale: the flicker occurs only with the fixture above or level with the blades (retail guidance, INFERENCE); the Building and Environment study exists but was not fetched (UNVERIFIED). Ship as an image cue, not as a fact claim.
