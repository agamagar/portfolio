# Airplane views · research dossier
_Researched 2026-08-15. Sources listed at the bottom, numbered; cite by number inline._

## What the medium actually is

There are three distinct real-world media stacked under this style system, and they have different names, different producers and different rules.

**1. The general-arrangement / 3-view drawing (the engineering artefact).** In aircraft drafting, orthographic projection is the method that "displays objects through multiple views" because several views are necessary to show the exact size and shape of a complex object; the alternative family is pictorial (perspective, isometric, oblique, exploded) which looks like a photograph but where "actual angles and dimensions are not accurate" [2]. Multiview orthographic projection gives the standard trio: **plan** (from directly above), **elevation** (vertical side view showing heights and external configuration) and **section** (a view through a cut plane, traditionally crosshatched) [3]. Arrangement is standardised: the front view is the principal view, and side views sit on the side they are named for [3]. Which side they sit on depends on **first-angle** (Europe, Asia) vs **third-angle** (USA, Japan, Australia, Canada) projection, flagged on real drawings by a truncated-cone symbol [3]. This is not academic for aviation: WWII British aircraft drawings had to be converted to third angle before American manufacturers could build from them [3].

Boeing publishes exactly this artefact for every model: 3-view drawings in "plan, side and front views" as AutoCAD .dwg/.dxf, "accurate within +/- 6 inches", produced for airport gate planning, covering 787-8/9/10, the whole 777 family including 777-8F/9, 737-200 through MAX 10, 747s, 767, 757, and heritage 707/DC-8/DC-9/DC-10/MD types [11]. The Airplane Characteristics for Airport Planning documents these belong to publish the drawings at stated scales such as 1:500 and 1:1000 [10, search-level]. _(inference)_ That means "Boeing airport-planning 3-view" is a real, citable register a prompt can name, and it implies a specific look: unshaded outline linework, dimension callouts, no livery, no ground, no sky.

**2. Line convention is a real hierarchy, not a stylistic choice.** ISO 128 is the international standard for "the general principles of presentation in technical drawings"; Part 2 is basic conventions for lines, Part 3 views, sections and cuts [12]. ISO 128-2 defines 15 basic line types plus three subtypes, and the working rule is a **2:1 thick-to-thin contrast ratio**: visible outlines at 0.5 to 0.6 mm, with hidden, centre and dimension lines at 0.25 to 0.3 mm [13, search-level]. The teaching table is: visible/object line thick (0.7 mm), hidden thin dashed (0.3 mm), centre thin, dimension thin, extension thin, cutting plane thick dashed, section lines thin diagonal, break lines, phantom thin dashed for range of motion [14]. Aircraft drawing practice matches: reference planes as long-dash-two-short-dashes, hidden detail dashed, centre lines long-dash-short-dash, and construction lines kept very light so they do not clutter [1, search-level]. **This ratio is the single most transferable fact into a prompt.**

**3. The spotter's recognition chart (the perception artefact).** The dominant system is **WEFT** · Wing, Engine, Fuselage, Tail · developed by Chief Observer C.H. Gibbs-Smith in WWII, adopted by the US Army Air Forces' Aircraft Recognition Unit in 1941 and still used as the US military mnemonic [8, search-level]. The 1942 US Army WEFT poster, illustrated by Jack Coggins for the Army Orientation Course, breaks aircraft into those four structural elements so a viewer can identify systematically rather than holistically [9]. Crucially, WEFT training "typically used silhouettes of only three-plan views (bottom view, head-on view, side view)" [8, search-level]. So a recognition chart is a **three-view silhouette set, not a three-view line drawing** · the plan view is from *below*, not above, because that is what an observer on the ground sees. That is a genuinely different convention from the engineering GA drawing and worth keeping distinct in the prompt system.

**4. The airliner profile illustration (the commercial artefact).** The reference practitioner here is Norebbo, who makes "blank illustration templates": side-profile illustrations of specific types on a blank white background, supplied with and without landing gear, sold as editable PSD and vector at around 5000 x 3000 px, explicitly as a base layer for livery design [15, 16]. The illustrator describes making a **"technical line drawing" alongside the profile views**, and frames the work as accuracy labour: "it requires a lot of effort to make sure that I'm illustrating these buggers as accurately as possible", with landing-gear proportions redone after the first pass against reference photos [16]. That is the exact register style (a) is imitating, and it validates the house no-livery rule: the professional version of this artefact *is* blank by design.

## What separates a convincing result from a generic one

**The accuracy markers, family by family.** These are the named features found in the spotting literature. Only these are cited; anything not listed here was not verified.

*Narrowbodies.*
- **Boeing 737**: sharper, more pointed nose, near-triangular in side profile [18, 19]. Cockpit windows angular, main windshield panels slope down to a point at the nose with visible creases between glass panels [18]. **Engine nacelle flattened on the bottom** (not circular) because the aircraft sits low · described as the single most reliable 737-vs-A320 discriminator [18]. Vertical fin has a sharper sweep at the root transitioning to a shallower angle [19]. Tail cone chisel-like [19]. Wingtips: clean on 737 Original, classic upswept on Classics, blended on NG, split scimitar as an NG/900ER retrofit, and **AT (Advanced Technology) winglets · dual blade, one up one down · exclusive to the MAX** [17, 19].
- **Airbus A320 family**: rounder, more bulbous, relatively blunt nose [18, 19, 22]. **Six cockpit windows, with the top rear corner of the last side window slightly cut off** [22]; near-vertical, nearly rectangular panels with a straighter lower edge and a notched rear corner [18, 19]. Round nacelles hanging conventionally below the wing [18]. Small curved dorsal fin; APU exhaust in a **rounded tail cone** [22]. Taller, more level ground stance [19]. Wingtips: none on early A320-100, wingtip fence (part up, part down) on ceo, tall upward-only sharklets at about 45 degrees on neo [17, 19, 22]. Engines: CFM56 has separate fan and core exhausts, V2500 a single exhaust nacelle, neo engines (LEAP-1A, PW1000G) noticeably larger diameter with visible thrust-reverser guidance rails [22]. **Length and exit ladder**: A318/A319 one emergency exit per side, A320 two overwing exits per side, A321 large exits fore and aft of the wing nearly as big as normal passenger doors; A318 → A319 (+2.5 m) → A320 (+4 m) → A321 longest [22].
- **Airbus A220**: curved nose likened to a de Havilland Comet; a small "cat flap" at the bottom of the tailplane [20].

*Widebodies.*
- **Boeing 767**: rounded, symmetrical, tapered tail cone with no distinctive features [21]; conical tail end [20]. **Four wheels per main bogie, tilted forward** [21]. One overwing exit per side on the -200, typically two on the -300 [23, search-level]. Some variants blended winglets, some raked [21].
- **Boeing 777**: **six wheels per main gear leg in a 3x2 arrangement, tilted rearward** · called the single most reliable distinguisher from a 767 [19, 21]. **Chisel-shaped tail cone with the APU exhaust port on the left side**, bladed or sawn rather than conical [20, 21]. No winglets at all on standard variants, just dramatically swept raked wingtips; folding wingtips on the 777X [17, 19, 21]. Very large nacelles (GE90-115B, 128 in fan diameter, wider than a 737 fuselage) [21]. Four main cabin doors per side on the -200, five on the -300/300ER [23, search-level]. Note the trap: 767 and 777 share almost identical nose sections and cockpit windows, so the nose is an unreliable identifier between them [21].
- **Boeing 787**: four large cockpit windows, smooth nose [19]; **chevrons (scalloped/serrated) on the nacelle trailing edge** [19, 20]; raked wingtips and highly flexible composite wings that curve up in flight [19]; slim swept "shark-fin" wing planform [20].
- **Airbus A330**: **four wheels per main leg, tilted rearward**; conical tail cone; small canted winglets on the ceo, larger curved devices on the neo; straight-bottomed cockpit windows [19, 20, 21].
- **Airbus A350**: the black cockpit "mask" wrapping six curved windows; large curved wingtips; four wheels per leg on the -900, six on the -1000 [19].
- **Boeing 747 / 747-8**: four engines; the 747-8 carries chevron ("cake-cutter") nacelle edges and partially raked wingtips, where the 747-400 has classic upswept winglets [17, 20].
- **A380**: four engines, wingtip fences, and a fuselage mass so large the wings read as bending downward from a head-on view [17, 20].

**The three things that most reliably make a result look generic.**
1. **A generic airliner.** If no WEFT-level features are named, the model averages 737 and A320 into a plausible non-aircraft. Nose profile, nacelle cross-section, wingtip device and wheel count are the four levers that pin a family.
2. **Perspective creeping into a supposedly orthographic view.** Vanishing lines on the fuselage, a foreshortened far wing, a visible far-side engine. The literature's fix is to name orthographic projection explicitly and add "without perspective" [5].
3. **Line-weight collapse.** Guides specifically flag that saying "thick and thin" is not enough and that numeric mm values are what hold the hierarchy [5].

**Studio-render craft for the all-white style.** A clay render replaces every material with a single neutral surface so the viewer reads form, proportion and shadow rather than texture [4, 6]. The material spec is mid-grey at roughly 0.18 in linear space (18% photographic grey) with a small amount of surface roughness so there are no harsh reflections [6]. Lighting aims at balanced, uniform illumination rather than drama: overcast HDRI, moderate sun-sky, subtle bounce [6]. Ambient occlusion is not physically required in a path-traced clay render because global illumination already produces contact shadows, but "light and controlled" AO is used in practice to enhance spatial depth [6]. Clay is a review artefact, used in early and mid phases to approve light, composition and positioning before materials are computed [4, 6]. The photographic analogue is the white cyclorama: a curved seamless wall lit overhead by softboxes for the "infinity" look, with the subject moved far from the wall · a rule of thumb of double the height of the tallest subject · so shadows dissipate before reaching the backdrop, and side-positioned directional lights added for subject definition instead of head-on light [7].

_(inference)_ Two of those transfer straight into prompt clauses that the current phrasing probably lacks: the **subject-to-backdrop distance** as the reason there is no cast shadow on the wall, and **AO as an explicit named pass** to keep panel lines and gear bays from vanishing into white-on-white.

## Model-side levers

Specific to Nano Banana / Gemini-class models, from the vendor guide and orthographic tutorials:

- **Write full-sentence natural language, not tag soup.** The official guidance is explicit: full sentences with proper grammar, act like a creative director, and give the model the "why" so it can make logical artistic decisions [24]. Keyword lists like `4k, realistic` are called out as the wrong pattern [24].
- **Materiality is a named lever.** The guide singles out describing surfaces as "matte finish", "brushed steel", "soft velvet" [24] · directly usable for the clay style.
- **Edit conversationally instead of re-rolling** when a result is roughly 80% right [24]. That argues for the prompt library shipping a short list of follow-up correction phrasings alongside each base prompt.
- **Consistency clauses work**: the documented pattern is "keep the person's facial features exactly the same as Image 1" while varying angle [24]. _(inference)_ The same construction should hold an aircraft type constant across a multi-view turnaround.
- **No documented negative-prompt syntax and no documented aspect-ratio syntax** in the official guide [24]. Constraints have to be phrased positively or as in-sentence prohibitions.
- **Orthographic tutorials converge on a specific vocabulary set**: "orthographic projection", "strict CAD linework with variable line weights", "plan view / elevation view / section view", plus numeric weights · 0.9 mm cut structure, 0.7 mm cut elements, 0.35 to 0.5 mm visible outlines, 0.25 mm dash-dot axes and centre lines, 0.18 mm hidden/projection dashes [5].
- **Suppress perspective with explicit phrasing**, e.g. "drawing only for the main structure without perspective", plus "add a ground line" [25, search-level], and demand "baseline consistency" across views [5].
- **Multi-view sheets need the layout spelled out.** The advice is to specify the grid arrangement explicitly, e.g. plan top-left, front elevation top-right, section bottom, rather than hoping for automatic alignment [5].
- **Documented failure modes to write against**: dimensional precision drifts 5 to 10 percent, so the drawing is not construction-grade; annotations get dropped unless requested; scale is inconsistent across views unless stated per view; line-weight hierarchy collapses without numbers [5].
- **Resolution**: 2048 x 2048 for A3-equivalent, 4096 x 4096 for A1, requested explicitly in the prompt [5].
- "Technical architectural font for annotations" is recommended to avoid stylised lettering [5]. _(inference)_ Under a no-branding house rule this is the safe way to get labels that look drafted without inviting a registration or airline name.

**Where research turned up nothing solid.** There is no published, citable convention document for aircraft 3-view SVG drawing style · the Wikimedia Commons category of 479 such files carries no written guidance on layout, silhouette vs line, scale or line weight, and the files themselves vary widely [26]. There is also no aviation profile illustrator process writeup found that walks through panel-line or control-surface-break method in detail; Norebbo's pages state the accuracy discipline and the existence of a companion technical line drawing but do not describe technique [15, 16]. Claims about panel-line and control-surface-break drawing conventions are therefore left out rather than invented. FM 44-80 (the US visual aircraft recognition field manual) and the Military Wiki aircraft-recognition article could not be fetched, so the WEFT detail here rests on the Smithsonian/UNT poster record and search-level summaries [8, 9].

## Proposed clause upgrades

1. **Replace** any generic "technical drawing" phrasing with: _"a general-arrangement orthographic drawing in the manner of a manufacturer airport-planning 3-view: plan, side elevation and front elevation, parallel projection, no perspective and no vanishing points."_ Reason: "general arrangement", "orthographic", "elevation" are the real artefact's names and orthographic is the documented term for suppressing convergence [2, 3, 5, 11].

2. **Add** an explicit line-weight clause: _"strict CAD linework with a 2:1 weight hierarchy · 0.6 mm continuous thick for visible outlines, 0.3 mm thin for hidden dashed lines, dimension lines and leaders, 0.25 mm long-dash-short-dash for the fuselage centre line."_ Reason: guides document that "thick and thin" alone causes line-weight collapse and that numeric mm values hold the hierarchy [5, 13, 14].

3. **Add** a negative-space clause phrased positively: _"no construction lines, no shading, no hatching outside section views, no ground shadow."_ Reason: aircraft drafting practice keeps construction lines very light precisely so they do not clutter, and the model has no documented negative-prompt syntax so prohibitions must sit inside the sentence [1, 24].

4. **Add** a WEFT clause to every technical-drawing prompt: _"identify the type by its wing planform, engine mounting and nacelle cross-section, fuselage length and exit arrangement, and tail shape."_ Reason: WEFT is the actual recognition system and naming the four elements front-loads the features models most often average away [8, 9].

5. **Replace** any bare aircraft name with a name plus two or three discriminators, e.g. _"Boeing 737 MAX: pointed nose with creased windshield panels, engine nacelle flattened along its lower lip, dual-blade AT winglet with one fin up and one down"_ or _"Airbus A320neo: blunt rounded nose, six cockpit windows with the rear side window's top corner cut off, circular nacelle, tall upward-only sharklet"_. Reason: these are the named, verified discriminators from the spotting literature [17, 18, 19, 22].

6. **Add** a countable-detail clause for widebodies: _"six wheels per main gear leg in a three-by-two bogie tilted rearward, chisel-cut tail cone with the APU exhaust on the left"_ for the 777, or _"four wheels per main leg tilted forward, conical tail cone"_ for the 767. Reason: wheel count and tailcone profile are described as the most reliable discriminators, and countable features are checkable at review [19, 21].

7. **Add** a wingtip-device clause drawn from the named taxonomy: _"raked wingtip, no upward extension, the wing simply tapering at a sharp highly swept angle"_ / _"wingtip fence, flat and projecting both up and slightly down"_ / _"blended winglet curving up from the wing with no sharp joint"_. Reason: the taxonomy is named and family-mapped, and "winglet" unqualified is the single commonest wrong detail [17].

8. **Replace** "white render" in style (b) with the practitioner term plus its material spec: _"a clay render: every material replaced with one neutral matte surface at 18 percent mid-grey, slight surface roughness so there are no harsh reflections."_ Reason: clay render is the actual studio term and the 0.18-linear plus roughness spec is what stops glossy white plastic [4, 6].

9. **Add** the lighting rig by instrument name: _"lit on a white cyclorama by a large overhead softbox with a side-positioned directional key for edge definition, the aircraft set far from the backdrop so its shadow falls off before reaching the wall."_ Reason: this is the documented shadowless cyc technique and it explains the absence of a cast shadow rather than just forbidding one [7].

10. **Add** an ambient-occlusion clause: _"a light, controlled ambient-occlusion pass darkening panel gaps, gear bays and the wing-to-fuselage fillet so form reads on a white body."_ Reason: AO is not physically required in a path-traced clay render but is used in practice specifically for spatial depth, which is exactly the white-on-white legibility problem [6].

11. **Add** a sheet-layout clause when more than one view is requested: _"lay the sheet out as a fixed grid · plan top-left, side elevation top-right, front elevation bottom-left · all three at one common scale and sharing a baseline."_ Reason: multi-view alignment and per-view scale are documented failure modes unless the grid is stated [5].

12. **Add** a distinct clause for the recognition-chart mode, separate from the GA drawing: _"a spotter's recognition chart: three filled black silhouettes on a plain field · from below, head-on and from the side · no interior detail."_ Reason: WEFT training used exactly that trio of three-plan silhouettes, and the bottom view (not the top) is what makes it read as a recognition chart rather than an engineering drawing [8, 9].

13. **Add** a consistency clause for turnarounds: _"keep the aircraft type, proportions and wingtip device exactly the same as the previous image; change only the viewing angle."_ Reason: the vendor guide documents this construction for cross-image consistency [24].
