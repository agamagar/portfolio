## What the medium actually is

### Stamp
A stamp is a small sheet-printed rectangle whose defining physical feature is how it was separated from its neighbours. Separation is measured as a **perforation gauge**: the number of holes in a 2 cm span of edge, historically 2 to 18 but in modern practice **11 to 14** [1]. Gauges are often **compound**, different vertically and horizontally (US stamps commonly perf 10.5 x 11) [1], which means the teeth along the top of a stamp are not necessarily the same pitch as the teeth down the side. That asymmetry is a cheap authenticity tell.

Two perforating methods dominate and they leave different corners. **Comb perforation** punches three sides of a stamp at once and repeats down the sheet, so rows line up cleanly and corners are regular [2]. **Line perforation** punches one row at a time, so the horizontal and vertical lines cross unevenly and corners are ragged, with holes that overlap or miss [2]. A convincing generated stamp needs one of these logics, not a decorative dotted outline: real perfs are holes with paper actually removed, leaving fibrous **teeth** between them. **Rouletting** is the related but distinct treatment where the paper is pierced or slit and no paper is removed [2].

Other physical facts worth holding: **gum** is the adhesive coating on the back, which cracks and can distort the paper [2]; **selvage** is the marginal paper of the sheet, sometimes carrying printer's marks [2]; a **cancellation** is the mark applied to void the stamp [2]. Printing is often **intaglio**, where the ink sits in lines cut *below* the plate surface [2] and therefore transfers as a slightly raised, ridged ink film on the paper, with line engraving and gravure both counting as intaglio [2]. That raised-line quality, plus the fact that engraved work is built from ruled and cross-hatched lines rather than tone, is what makes an engraved stamp read as engraved. Since this library forbids text and numerals in the image, what survives as signal is: the perforated edge with correct gauge and method, the teeth, the paper fibre in the tooth, engraved line texture, gum sheen on a lifted corner, and an off-white or lightly toned stock. The **denomination panel** still exists as a *form* (a reserved block or cartouche in the layout) even with no number in it _(inference)_.

### Postcard
Postcards are dated and identified by physical traits more than by imagery: card stock weight, texture and colour; the printing process (chromolithography, halftone dot, offset litho); and back formatting [12]. The **divided back**, a vertical rule splitting message from address, arrives in 1907 and is the single most recognisable back-side structure [12]. The **white border era** (1915 to 1930) put a printed white margin around the image to save ink [12]. The **linen era** (1930 to 1945) used a textured, fabric-like surface with bright offset-litho colour [12]. The **photochrome era** (1945 onward) is glossy and photographic [12]. **Postmarks and cancellations** are the dating evidence on the back [12].

For Specimen this gives three usable stock characters: a matte, slightly warm linen-textured card with visible cross-hatch tooth; a glossy photochrome card with a specular sheen and a printed white border; and a heavier pioneer-era board. Deckled versus cut edges: no source fetched confirmed deckle-edge postcard practice, so treat the deckle as an optional _(inference)_ paper-making artefact (a soft, feathered, fibrous edge) rather than a documented postcard convention.

### 35mm slide
The film frame is **24 x 36 mm**, aspect 2:3 [14]. The film strip carries **perforations** on both long edges at Kodak Standard pitch, eight perforations per frame over 38.00 mm, with 2 mm gaps between frames [14]. So a strip has sprocket holes, frame lines, and interframe gaps, all at a fixed rhythm; getting that rhythm wrong is the usual generated-image failure.

A mounted slide is that frame trapped in a **2 x 2 inch** card or plastic mount, the film being placed in a small square of cardboard or a plastic shell for protection [source: slide mount survey, 6]. Mounts have recognisable furniture: striped mount designs, embossed or stamped markings, coloured borders (predominantly red or black), corner logos, and occasionally rounded corners [6]. Kodachrome mounts ran roughly 1945 to 1982; Ektachrome-processed mounts paralleled Kodachrome's styles with **blue replacing the red** [6]. The **aperture window** is the cut opening in the mount, slightly smaller than the film frame so the mount masks the edges. No fetched source gave an exact window dimension, so treat "window slightly under 24 x 36" as _(inference)_.

Slide film is **reversal** film: a positive image on a transparent base, viewed by projection, on a lightbox with a loupe, or scanned [5]. It has **higher contrast and finer grain** than negative film, and **fewer stops of exposure latitude** [5]. That is the whole visual argument for backlighting a slide: highlights clip early, shadows go dense and near-black, and the image glows as transmitted rather than reflected light. **Kodachrome** (1935 to 2009, K-14 process) is non-substantive, with dye couplers added during processing rather than sitting in the emulsion, giving thinner layers, less light scatter and sharper recording [4]; its rendering was described as having "more poetry in it, a softness, an elegance" against the more electric Velvia [4]. It is exceptionally stable in the dark (yellow dye, the least stable, losing 20% in 185 years) but weak under projection, fading in about an hour against Fujichrome's 2.5 hours [4]. **Ektachrome** is the simpler E-6 reversal stock [4][5]. Practical translation: Kodachrome for restrained, slightly warm, deep-red-and-dense-black rendering; Ektachrome for cooler, cleaner, more neutral transparency.

Held up to a window versus laid on a lightbox is a real difference: on a lightbox the illumination is even, colour-corrected and edge-to-edge, so the mount is lit from behind and reads as a dark silhouette with a glowing window; held up, the light source is uncontrolled and the mount catches front light too _(inference, extrapolated from the viewing methods listed in [5])_.

### Polaroid
Integral instant film puts every layer into one self-contained unit that develops and fixes without intervention [13]. A **pod of viscous alkaline reagent** at the trailing edge is ruptured by the camera's rollers and spread between the negative and the receiving positive sheet [13]. Colour works by **dye diffusion transfer** through three emulsion layers, the dye migrating to the positive surface to form the image [13]. Development timing varies with ambient temperature [13].

The geometry is the recognisable part: the white border with a **wide chin** exists because the image occupies a smaller area inside the frame [13], and the chin is wide specifically because that is where the pod and its spread reagent live _(inference, from the pod's position at the trailing edge [13])_. SX-70 and 600 image area is **3.1 x 3.1 in (79 x 79 mm)** square; Spectra/Image is 3.6 x 2.9 in (91 x 74 mm) [13]. The outer frame dimension of SX-70 (commonly quoted as 3.5 x 4.25 in) appeared only in search summaries of a source that would not serve, so treat it as UNVERIFIED and specify the ratio behaviourally instead: a square window, thin and equal borders on left, right and top, and a chin roughly twice to three times those [_(inference)_].

The look follows from the chemistry: dye diffusion through emulsion gives soft edges rather than lens-sharp ones, lower resolving power, and a characteristic muted, slightly cool or slightly warm cast depending on stock and temperature [13]. Because the emulsion stays mobile during development the layer can be physically pushed around, which is the basis of emulsion-manipulation art [source: SX-70 development window, per search summaries; the underlying mobility is implied by [13]'s diffusion description]. Edge bleed and uneven pod spread at the image corners come from the roller spread being imperfect _(inference)_.

### Foil card
A holographic foil is a metallised film with a **diffraction grating embossed into the release coat at 1,000 to 1,400 lines/mm**, with a surface relief **0.5 to 2 microns deep**, acting as thousands of microscopic prisms that split white light into spectral colour [3]. The rainbow is **diffraction, not pigment**: different wavelengths reflect at different angles, so the rainbow *moves* as the viewing angle changes [3]. Pattern repeat runs **2 to 50 mm**, which sets the perceived scale of the effect [3]. Pattern families are generic repeating motifs (stars, dots, grids), stock 2D/3D motifs like waves or prisms, and custom origination [3]. **Hot stamping** presses a heated die to transfer the foil; the grating is fragile, so press nip pressure is reduced 5 to 8% specifically to avoid flattening it [3], and minimum line width is around 80 microns [3]. CMYK can overprint the foil at 70 to 85% ink density and still preserve the diffraction [3], which is exactly the "artwork sitting on top of a shifting rainbow" look of a foil trading card.

Two prompt-relevant distinctions follow from [3]: a **linear** grating gives banded rainbow sweeps that run in one direction and flip as you tilt; a **prismatic or pattern** grating gives a repeating field of small rainbow cells at the 2 to 50 mm repeat. Trading-card **refractor** construction was not verifiable: the one source on it returned 403, so nothing here is cited to it and refractor-specific claims should be treated as UNVERIFIED.

### Studio light
The three variables to name are **direction** (shapes texture and depth), **intensity** (exposure and mood) and **quality**, where hard light gives crisp shadows suited to jewellery and soft light suits cosmetics [7]. Setups scale: one light at 45 degrees with a white foam-board reflector; two lights at opposing 45s; three-point with key, fill and back/rim for dimensional separation, which is the studio standard for hero shots [7]. Category logic matters: jewellery wants hard focused light for sparkle, beauty wants soft even light, texture wants side or back light [7].

Modifier vocabulary, defined: **key light** is the primary source; **fill** reduces the darkness of the shadows the key creates; **softbox** softens output; a **grid** on a softbox focuses the diffused light onto the subject; a **snoot** is a narrow cone giving a spotlight; a **gobo** is a shaped disc or sheet placed in front of a light to cast a patterned light or shadow; **flats** are large black boards that stop light spilling back onto the subject; **diffusion** is material light passes through to soften and widen it [8]. A **scrim** is fabric with the source behind it, emitting diffuse light much like a softbox, or a wire mesh that cuts intensity without changing colour temperature [source: search-level definition, see 7's neighbours; treat the mesh sense as UNVERIFIED]. **Strip boxes** are long narrow softboxes producing a narrow beam, ideal for backlighting a subject into a rim [source: search-level summary, UNVERIFIED]. **Negative fill** and **negative bounce** are black surfaces creating a dark "highlight" on light objects to separate the product from the background [source: search-level summary drawn from the same lighting-glossary cluster; treat as UNVERIFIED as a phrase, though "flats stop spill" is confirmed by [8]].

Film and grain vocabulary, since this style wants grain. Grain is the visible texture from the random distribution and size of **silver halide crystals**, or the **dye clouds** developed from them, in the emulsion [9]. Faster film needs larger crystals, so higher ISO means larger, more pronounced grain [9]; fine grain resolves more but needs more light [9]. Grain is quantified as **RMS granularity**, root-mean-square fluctuation in optical density measured through a 0.048 mm aperture [9]. Grain becomes objectionable mainly through **over-enlargement** [9], which is the key lever: grain scale is a function of magnification, not of the subject. Critically, **digital noise is random coloured specks, while film grain is monochromatic** and related to image content and light distribution [11]; real grain "exists in layers within the emulsion" and reads three-dimensional [11]. Descriptive register that works: creamy (Tri-X), crunchy (HP5), smooth as butter (Portra 160), aggressively gritty (pushed Delta 3200) [11]. **Halation**, the red glow around bright light sources, comes from Cinestill 800T having the remjet layer removed [source: search-level summary of a film review; UNVERIFIED as fetched, so use the visual description rather than asserting the mechanism]. Push processing amplifies existing grain rather than creating new grain [source: same search cluster, UNVERIFIED].

## What separates a convincing result from a generic one

**One separation logic, followed all the way round.** A generic stamp has evenly spaced circles. A convincing one commits to comb or line perforation and shows the consequence: comb gives regular corners, line gives uneven crossings and irregular corners [2]. Pick one per colourway and say which.

**Compound gauge.** Real stamps frequently perf differently on the two axes [1]. Specifying, for example, "perf 13 horizontally, 12.5 vertically" produces an edge that is subtly non-uniform in the way real ones are.

**Paper removed, not paper printed.** Perforation removes paper; rouletting does not [2]. If the brief does not say holes with fibrous teeth, the model tends to draw a scalloped outline.

**The chin is caused, not decorative.** The Polaroid chin is wide because the pod and its roller-spread reagent sit at the trailing edge [13]. State the cause and the border proportions land; state only "white border" and the model centres the image.

**Transmitted versus reflected light.** A slide on a lightbox is lit from behind, so it clips highlights and blocks up shadows because reversal film has narrow latitude [5]. A slide held in the hand gets both. Deciding which, and saying so, is the difference between a glowing transparency and a small photo printed on a card.

**The rainbow must be angular.** Diffraction shifts with viewing angle [3]. A single static rainbow gradient reads as a filter. A rainbow that runs one direction across the card, brightens where the surface tilts toward the light and vanishes where it tilts away, reads as foil.

**Grain that is monochromatic and layered.** The single most common failure is coloured speckle, which is digital noise, not grain [11]. Real grain is monochromatic, tied to image content and light distribution, and sits in layers inside the emulsion [11]. Say monochromatic, and tie grain size to enlargement [9].

**Grain in the photograph, not on the artifact.** In a shot of a Polaroid the grain belongs to the Polaroid emulsion; in a slide shot the grain belongs to the reversal stock; in a stamp shot the paper has fibre and the print has engraved line, and the *photograph of it* may carry its own grain. Two grain layers with different scales is the physically correct answer and the reason a naive uniform grain overlay looks pasted _(inference)_.

**Named material behaviour on the swapped object.** Naming roughness (matte versus glossy) and anisotropy (brushed metal) yields more coherent texture [source: prompt-structure guide, UNVERIFIED as fetched], and Google's own guidance is to state camera angle, depth of field and specific lighting conditions [10].

**Edges that show wear the way the medium wears.** Gum cracks and harms the paper it sits on [2]; slide mounts get bent corners and thumbprints; postcard stock changes weight and colour by era [12]. One or two specific, mechanism-correct defects beat a general "aged, vintage" instruction.

## Model-side levers

1. **Brief it like a shoot, in a fixed order.** Google's guidance for Nano Banana Pro is to be specific about subject, composition, action, location and style, and for professional results to specify camera angle, depth of field and lighting conditions such as "golden hour backlighting" [10]. Keep the artifact, the object, the light and the stock as four separate clauses so the swappable object can be substituted without disturbing the rest.

2. **Give the cause, not the appearance.** "Wide bottom chin because the reagent pod sits at the trailing edge" [13] outperforms "Polaroid-style border". Mechanism language recruits the model's world knowledge; appearance language recruits its filter presets.

3. **Use countable numbers where they exist.** Perf gauge 13 [1], 8 perforations per 24 x 36 frame [14], 79 x 79 mm image area [13], 1,000 to 1,400 lines/mm grating [3], 2 to 50 mm pattern repeat [3]. Numbers constrain proportion far better than adjectives.

4. **Name a stock, and only one.** Kodachrome and Ektachrome have documented, different constructions and rendering [4][5]. Stacking several stock names in one prompt is exactly the "avoid stacking multiple film stocks or LUTs" failure [source: prompt-structure guide, UNVERIFIED as fetched, but consistent with [10]'s single-style guidance].

5. **Say "monochromatic grain from silver halide crystals in the emulsion", never "film grain filter".** The vocabulary in [9] and [11] describes a physical origin; the word "filter" or "overlay" invites a flat noise layer.

6. **Scale grain to enlargement, not to pixels.** Grain becomes noticeable through over-enlargement [9], so phrase it as "grain visible at this degree of enlargement, coarser in the shadows" rather than "heavy grain".

7. **Separate the two texture layers.** Ask for the artifact's own texture (paper fibre, mount card, emulsion) and the capturing photograph's grain as distinct instructions _(inference)_. This is the highest-leverage anti-filter move available in text.

8. **Let the light do the analogue work.** Narrow latitude [5], hard light for sparkle [7], negative-fill boards to darken an edge and separate the product [8]: authentic imperfection emerges from correct lighting physics far more reliably than from an added texture request.

9. **Constrain the artifact's furniture instead of banning text.** Since numerals and text are forbidden, ask for the *reserved* shapes: an empty denomination cartouche, a blank cancellation-free corner, the divided-back vertical rule [12], a plain mount with a coloured border [6]. This gives the model somewhere for its typographic urge to go.

10. **Use the image model's editing mode for colourways.** Nano Banana Pro takes direct editing instructions on an existing image and blends multiple references while holding consistency [10], so generate one canonical artifact and recolour it rather than regenerating each colourway from scratch.

## Proposed clause upgrades

1. Replace any "perforated stamp edge" phrasing with: **"comb-perforated on all four sides, gauge 13 horizontally and 12.5 vertically, paper actually punched away leaving fibrous teeth between the holes."** Compound gauges and true holes are how real stamps separate [1][2], and this removes the scalloped-outline failure.

2. Add for the second stamp colourway: **"line-perforated instead, so the horizontal and vertical rows cross unevenly and the four corners are irregular."** Line perforation's uneven crossings are documented [2] and give a second, distinguishable stamp variant without new geometry.

3. Add to the stamp print description: **"intaglio line engraving, image built from ruled and cross-hatched engraved lines sitting in slight relief above the paper."** Intaglio is ink from below the plate surface [2], so relief is the correct optical result and it steers away from flat digital print.

4. Add one physical stamp defect: **"a lifted corner showing dried gum on the reverse, faintly crazed."** Gum cracks and harms the paper [2], so this is a mechanism-correct defect rather than generic distressing.

5. Replace "Polaroid-style white border" with: **"79 x 79 mm square image area inside a white frame with equal thin borders left, right and top and a chin at the bottom two to three times deeper, because the reagent pod sits at the trailing edge."** The image area and pod position are documented [13]; the ratio is _(inference)_ but the cause is not.

6. Add to the Polaroid clause: **"soft, migrated edges and muted colour from dye diffusion transfer through three emulsion layers, with slightly uneven reagent spread at the corners."** Dye diffusion and roller spread are the actual mechanism [13], and this replaces "faded vintage look" with something the model can render specifically.

7. Replace "35mm slide" with: **"a 24 x 36 mm reversal frame in a 2 x 2 inch cardboard mount, the aperture window cut slightly smaller than the frame so the mount masks the film edges."** The frame size [14] and 2 x 2 inch card mount [6] are documented and this produces correctly nested rectangles.

8. Add to the backlit slide clause: **"lit from behind on an even lightbox, transmitted light only, highlights clipping early and shadows going dense because reversal film has few stops of latitude."** Narrow latitude is documented [5], and this is what makes a transparency read as transparent rather than as a printed card.

9. Colourway differentiator for the slide: **"red-bordered Kodachrome mount, thin-emulsion rendering with low scatter"** versus **"blue Ektachrome mount, cooler and more neutral."** Kodachrome and Ektachrome mounts differed by exactly this red/blue substitution [6], and Kodachrome's non-substantive thin layers are documented [4].

10. Replace any film-strip clause with: **"Kodak Standard perforations along both long edges, eight perforations per frame across 38 mm with a 2 mm interframe gap."** Exact sprocket rhythm [14] is the detail that fails most visibly at any crop.

11. Replace "holographic foil" with: **"hot-stamped diffraction foil, an embossed grating of roughly 1,200 lines per millimetre, the spectral rainbow sweeping across the surface with viewing angle rather than sitting still, in a repeating pattern about 8 mm across."** All three figures are documented [3] and they convert a static rainbow gradient into diffraction behaviour.

12. Add to the foil card: **"the artwork overprinted on the foil at reduced ink density so the grating still shows through in the printed areas."** CMYK at 70 to 85% density preserves diffraction [3], which is precisely the trading-card foil look of colour floating over a live rainbow.

13. Replace any "film grain" instruction with: **"monochromatic grain from silver halide crystals within the emulsion, layered rather than overlaid, coarser in the shadows, at a scale consistent with this degree of enlargement."** Grain is monochromatic and content-linked while digital noise is coloured speckle [11], and grain visibility scales with enlargement [9].

14. Add a two-layer texture clause: **"two distinct textures: the artifact's own surface (paper fibre, mount card, emulsion) and, separately, the grain of the photograph taken of it, at a coarser scale."** _(inference)_ built on [9] and [11]; it is the clearest available instruction against a single flat noise overlay.

15. Replace generic "studio lighting" with: **"three-point studio setup, softboxed key at 45 degrees, soft fill opposite, strip rim behind for separation, black negative-fill boards close on the shadow side to darken the product edge."** Three-point is the documented studio standard for hero shots [7] and flats stopping spill are documented [8]; the negative-fill phrasing is UNVERIFIED as a term but the behaviour is confirmed.

16. Add a material-matched light clause: **"hard focused light if the swapped object is reflective or faceted, soft even light if it is matte or cosmetic, side or back light if the point is surface texture."** Category-matched lighting quality is documented [7], and this makes the one style adapt correctly to any swapped object.
