# Emotion in a still, and the model-side colour and mood levers : research dossier
_Researched 2026-09-04. Sources numbered at the bottom; cite by number inline._

Reading key: **VENDOR** = in OpenAI or Google documentation. **PRACTITIONER** = a working photographer or editor writing from tests or practice. **COMMUNITY** = prompt-sharing sites and style libraries; evidence of lore only. **INFERENCE** = my reasoning, not in any source. **UNVERIFIED** = a claim I could not confirm against a fetched text.

Where the text was fetched as a PDF (Barthes [1], Berger [2], Sontag [3], the GUP piece on Cartier-Bresson [4]) the quotes below are lifted from the extracted text; OCR artefacts were corrected only where the word was unambiguous. Em-dashes inside quoted texts have been replaced with commas or colons to honour the house rule; the wording is otherwise verbatim.

## Part A: what makes a still carry emotion (mechanisms, each with who named it and source #)

### A1. The detail that was not put there: punctum (Barthes) [1]
Barthes splits a photograph into **studium**, "that very wide field of unconcerned desire, of various interest, of inconsequential taste: I like / I don't like ... of the order of liking, not of loving", and **punctum**, the element that "rises from the scene, shoots out of it like an arrow, and pierces me" [1]. The studium is what the photographer intended and the culture reads: "the figures, the faces, the gestures, the settings, the actions" [1]. The punctum is nearly always a **small object or partial feature**: the "strapped pumps" and "belt worn low" on the woman in James Van Der Zee's family portrait, "one boy's bad teeth" in a Klein street picture, Tzara's "large hand ... resting on the door frame", the "spatulate nails" in a Warhol portrait, the collar and finger bandage on Lewis Hine's children [1]. Barthes: "A detail overwhelms the entirety of my reading; it is an intense mutation of my interest, a fulguration ... it is of no importance that its referent is insignificant" [1].

Two rules follow that matter for a prompt library:
- **It must not read as planted.** "Certain details may 'prick' me. If they do not, it is doubtless because the photographer has put them there intentionally ... the detail which interests me is not, or at least is not strictly, intentional, and probably must not be so; it occurs in the field of the photographed thing like a supplement that is at once inevitable and delightful" [1]. Deliberate contrast, he says, "produces no effect on me, except perhaps one of irritation" [1].
- **It resists naming.** "What I can name cannot really prick me. The incapacity to name is a good symptom of disturbance" [1]. Of a Mapplethorpe portrait: "The effect is certain but unlocatable, it does not find its sign, its name; it is sharp and yet lands in a vague zone of myself; it is acute yet muffled, it cries out in silence" [1].

INFERENCE: for a generator this means the tender prop must be described as a fact of the scene (a bandage, a low belt, a chipped mug), never as "a poignant detail". The moment the prompt labels the feeling, the studium swallows it.

### A2. The second look: the punctum arrives after the fact (Barthes) [1]
"Nothing surprising, then, if sometimes, despite its clarity, the punctum should be revealed only after the fact, when the photograph is no longer in front of me and I think back on it. I may know better a photograph I remember than a photograph I am looking at" [1]. His example: he thought the strapped pumps moved him; "later on I realized that the real punctum was the necklace she was wearing ... a slender ribbon of braided gold" that an aunt in his own family had worn [1]. He adds the mechanism of reception: "to say nothing, to shut my eyes, to allow the detail to rise of its own accord into affective consciousness", and, "Last thing about the punctum: whether or not it is triggered, it is an addition: it is what I add to the photograph and what is nonetheless already there" [1].

### A3. The blind field: what is just out of frame (Barthes) [1]
Barthes borrows Bazin's cinema "blind field" and says most photographs lack it: "confronting millions of photographs, including those which have a good studium, I sense no blind field: everything which happens within the frame dies absolutely once this frame is passed beyond ... they are anesthetized and fastened down, like butterflies. Yet once there is a punctum, a blind field is created (is divined)" [1]. Because of the necklace, the woman "has had, for me, a whole life external to her portrait" [1]. The Queen Victoria example: the studium is the queen on horseback; the punctum is the kilted groom holding the bridle, and the question it opens ("what if the horse suddenly began to rear?") [1]. He then generalises to the erotic versus the pornographic: the erotic photograph "may very well not show them at all; it takes the spectator outside its frame, and it is there that I animate this photograph and it animates me ... as if the image launched desire beyond what it permits us to see" [1]. The pornographic image, by contrast, is "like a shop window which shows only one illuminated piece of jewelry ... no secondary, untimely object ever manages to half conceal, delay, or distract" [1].

INFERENCE: the one-thing-illuminated shop window is the exact failure mode of an over-directed AI image. A frame that reads as emotional needs a secondary, untimely object, and an implied off-frame (a doorway, a second cup, a hand entering from the edge).

### A4. The pensive photograph, and the look held back (Barthes) [1]
"Ultimately, Photography is subversive not when it frightens, repels, or even stigmatizes, but when it is pensive, when it thinks" [1]; Life's editors rejected Kertesz because his images "spoke too much" [1]. On the gaze: the Kertesz boy holding a puppy "looks into the lens with his sad, jealous, fearful eyes ... In fact, he is looking at nothing; he retains within himself his love and his fear: that is the Look" [1]; "the look, eliding the vision, seems held back by something interior" [1]. And on gesture, his caption for the Mapplethorpe arm: "the hand at the right degree of openness, the right density of abandonment" [1].

### A5. The length of the quotation; gestures in isolation go mute (Berger) [2]
Berger: "Photographs do not translate from appearances. They quote from them" [2]. Every photograph carries "a shock of discontinuity ... Between the moment recorded and the present moment of looking at the photograph, there is an abyss" [2]; "Discontinuity always produces ambiguity" [2]. The photographer's lever is the choice of instant: "a photographer, through the choice of the instant photographed, may try to persuade the viewer to lend that instant a past and a future. Looking at the man with the horse, we have no clear idea of what has just happened or what is about to happen. Looking at the Kertesz, we can trace a story backwards for years and forwards for at least a few hours" [2]. The "length" of the quotation "is in no sense a temporal length. It is not time that is prolonged but meaning" [2].

On gesture, the warning that matters most: "It is not memorable, as most picture editors might assume, because the boy's expression and gestures are happy and charming. When isolated, photographed gestures and expressions become either mute or caricatural. Here, however, they are not isolated. They contain and are confronted by an idea" [2]. In the shepherd-boy picture the idea is touch: the fleece the hand is stroking and "the texture of the stubble on which the boy is rolling and which he must feel through his shirt" [2]. "A photograph which achieves expressiveness thus works dialectically: it preserves the particularity of the event recorded, and it chooses an instant when the correspondences of those particular appearances articulate a general idea" [2].

On the look that excludes the viewer (Kertesz, "A Red Hussar Leaving, 1919"): "The opposition exists in the parting look between the man and the woman. This look is not directed towards the viewer. We witness it as the older soldier with the moustache and the woman with the shawl (perhaps a sister) do ... he is watching his father, and yet he is excluded from their look" [2].

INFERENCE: a gesture becomes emotional when it is tethered to a texture or object it is acting on (a hand on fleece, a thumb on a mug rim), and when the look goes to someone in the frame rather than to the lens.

### A6. Pathos is the default state of a photograph (Sontag) [3]
"Photography is an elegiac art, a twilight art. Most subjects photographed are, just by virtue of being photographed, touched with pathos. An ugly or grotesque subject may be moving because it has been dignified by the attention of the photographer. A beautiful subject can be the object of rueful feelings, because it has aged or decayed or no longer exists. All photographs are memento mori" [3]. "The photograph is a thin slice of space as well as time. In a world ruled by photographic images, all borders ('framing') seem arbitrary" [3]. "The knowledge gained through still photographs will always be some kind of sentimentalism, whether cynical or humanist" [3]. And: "when we are nostalgic, we take pictures" [3].

INFERENCE: the prompt does not have to manufacture feeling; it has to stop cancelling it. Signs of time passing (wear, a dated fashion, a fading print) push on a lever the medium already has.

### A7. The decisive moment, and what it actually says (Cartier-Bresson) [4][5]
The 1952 preface: "photography is the simultaneous recognition, in a fraction of a second, of the significance of an event as well as of a precise organization of forms which give that event its proper expression" [4]. It is about cutting, not waiting: "It is essential ... to cut from the raw material of life, to cut and cut, but to cut with discrimination" [4]. Early on he "prowled the streets all day, feeling very strung-up and ready to pounce, determined to 'trap' life, to preserve life in the act of living" and "craved to seize the whole essence, in the confines of one single photograph, of some situation that was in the process of unrolling itself" [4]. "The picture-story involves a joint operation of the brain, the eye and the heart" [4]; elsewhere paraphrased as "the alignment of the head, heart and eye" [5]. Two other lines commonly attributed to the preface, "the smallest thing can be a great subject" and "putting one's head, one's eye and one's heart on the same axis", appear only on quote-aggregator pages in my search: UNVERIFIED as to exact wording. Cartier-Bresson also rejected colour for feeling: "Colour for me should be reserved for painting. I find emotion in black and white" [5], a useful counter-voice to A9.

### A8. The anti-decisive moment: frozen between moments (Frank, via Jno Cook) [6]
Cook (Exposure, 1986): "In The Americans, America stood still, frozen into a frightful pose between moments" [6]; "Frank brought to a close photography's quest for the decisive moment, the ever more decisive moment which had been defined in terms of the perishable and publishable moment" [6]. The pictures were "received instead as a visceral attack" [6], yet "anchored in a bedrock of specificity and careful intent. Each meant something, each was taken for a reason" [6]; "Frank's ability to build a series of single unrelated images to a crescendo of unnerving feelings is perhaps the most masterful aspect of the book" [6]. Contemporary attacks on "meaningless blur, grain, muddy exposures, drunken horizons" (Popular Photography) and Kerouac's "sad poem" introduction surfaced only in search summaries; the Kerouac text was not fetched: UNVERIFIED here.

INFERENCE: the between-moment is a distinct register from the decisive one: nobody is mid-action, the pose is slack, the face may be turned or blurred. It is closer to how a model naturally renders a "candid" and is worth naming explicitly.

### A9. Colour as feeling, and the tonic of restraint (Eggleston, Leiter) [7][8][9]
Eggleston: "I am at war with the obvious" and "I had this notion of what I called a democratic way of looking around, that nothing was more or less important" [7]; he forced himself to photograph erosion and weeds where "there was no picture there" [7]. On The Red Ceiling he said only that it is "so powerful that, in fact, I've never seen it reproduced on the page to my satisfaction" [8] (Wikipedia, citing the Getty). The widely repeated "a little red is usually enough" line was NOT in the fetched page: UNVERIFIED.

Leiter's palette is described as "muted, almost pastel-like" from expired Kodachrome, with "neon signs, umbrellas, and yellow taxis providing subdued chromatic accents", faces obscured, "reflections in wet surfaces and glass", "rain and steam obscuring street scenes" [9]. Leiter: "I like it when one is not certain what one sees" (Colors, 2011) [9]; "I believe there is such a thing as a search for beauty" (In No Great Hurry, 2013) [9].

INFERENCE: the two colourists agree on one operating rule: one saturated accent against a muted field, and the accent sits on an ordinary object (a taxi, a ceiling, an umbrella). "Vibrant" as a global adjective is the opposite of what they did.

### A10. The lit window as threshold, and adjusting colour for feeling (Hido) [10][11][12][13][14]
Hido: "Lights on in the windows gives you an indication that maybe there's life in there, and you imagine the stories of the people inside" [10]; "this could be the moment right before something important happens, or just after" [10]; "one of the joys of shooting at night or through the window of a car is that you don't always know what that frozen moment will bring" [10]; the cover of Intimate Distance shows "a small house with that specific interior blue light that means someone is watching TV" [10]. He wants people to "project their own hopes and fears and emotional colors onto these photographs" [10]. To Dazed: "I'm attracted to that cinematic feeling where something's about to happen. Kind of like a pregnant moment" [11]; "I shoot like a documentarian, but I print like a painter" [11]; "I really like what happens when you put a person with a place" [11]. Poynor records Hido on picture #2133, two windows "each illuminated by the blue light of a television", and that he "found it sad that the people sitting in the dark weren't watching something together, as though the windows alone amounted to proof of estrangement"; Hido: "I'm not married to reality. I don't feel I have to faithfully describe a place", having "adjusted the color for emotional effect while staying within the bounds of atmospheric credibility" [12]. To W: "There have always been themes of isolation and a sort of loneliness in my photographs" and "I wasn't taking pictures of places. I was sort of making pictures about people" [13]. The gallery text: "a singular lit window, a door left ajar or an empty car become signifiers of stories left untold"; the light is "a cinematic, halogen glow that is at once intense and warm, and unsettlingly eerie"; "lit with the site-specific artificial lighting that surrounds each house" [14].

Mechanisms: the single lit window as evidence of an unseen person; the specific colour of a light source as a narrative fact (TV blue = someone alone watching); mixed sodium/halogen and cold night as the loneliness pair; colour pushed for feeling but held inside "atmospheric credibility".

### A11. Loneliness as the failure to be alone; slowness of the big camera (Soth) [15]
"The work is really about the failure to be alone. The people that I photographed allowed me to photograph them because they didn't want to be alone. Nobody really wants to be alone. People need people" (Interview magazine, quoted in [15]). The large-format camera and tripod gave "slowness", letting him "observe subjects and embrace awkward silences" [15]. INFERENCE: the Soth portrait's emotion comes from a sitter who has agreed to be looked at and is holding still for it: stillness, direct but unguarded gaze, a lot of room around one figure.

### A12. Intimacy: the diary, the party, and love over sociology (Goldin, Sultan) [16][17]
Goldin on the Ballad: "the diary I let people read"; "My written diaries are private ... My visual diary is public"; "There is a popular notion that the photographer is by nature a voyeur, the last one invited to the party. But I'm not crashing: this is my party. This is my family, my history" [16]; "This work was always about reality, the hard truth, and there was never any artifice" [16]. Sultan on Pictures from Home: "What drives me to continue this work is difficult to name. It has more to do with love than with sociology"; "in the odd and jumbled process of working everything shifts; the boundaries blur, my distance slips, the arrogance and illusion of immunity falters"; "These are my parents. From that simple fact, everything follows"; "I want my parents to live forever" [17].

Mechanism: proximity that is social, not optical. The camera is inside the room by right. INFERENCE: in a prompt this shows as domestic props with wear, on-camera flash or lamp light rather than studio light, and subjects who ignore the camera because it belongs to someone they know.

### A13. What editors and juries say lands [18][19][20]
Kira Pollack, 2026 World Press Photo global jury chair: "the most powerful photographs are layered and emotional. They collapse the distance between the person in the frame and the person holding the page"; a winning frame is "dignified and restrained, and all the more powerful for it"; photography can "activate empathy, to make a viewer feel, even before they think, that something concerning is happening" [18]. The 2026 jury statement on Carol Guzy's winner stresses evidence over formal devices [19]. John Morris (Magnum, Life): "one first looks for the meaning ... The composition, the form, which is more of an aesthetic question, comes second"; "Good composition takes the eye to the focal point"; the editor needs "a sense of empathy" [20]. Nothing solid found on a Magnum or WPP editor naming "the moment before" as a rule; the closest is Hido's "pregnant moment" [11].

## Part B: model-side levers (vendor-documented / practitioner / community, clearly tiered)

### B1. VENDOR: what OpenAI documents [21][22][23]
- **Prompt order and register.** "Write prompts in a consistent order (background/scene → subject → key details → constraints)" [21]; state the intended use so the model "picks the right mode and polish level" [21]; "Use photography language (lens, lighting, framing)" [22].
- **Lighting and mood vocabulary used in the guides.** "soft diffuse, golden hour, high-contrast" [21]; "soft coastal daylight, shallow depth of field", "Soft cinematic lighting, shallow depth of field, tasteful bokeh lights", "warm and comforting mood", "dusk, with natural lighting and realistic colors" [21]; mood words in examples: "warm", "nostalgic", "gentle", "emotional", "cozy", "grounded", "authentic" [22].
- **Colour.** "natural color balance", "warm earthy colors", "strong color direction", "clear color direction" [21]; for edits, "Match lighting, shadows, and color temperature to the original photo" [21]. Colour temperature is named as a concept; **no Kelvin values** appear [21][22]. Saturation is not treated as a standalone control [21].
- **Documentary versus cinematic.** The grounded look is documented as an explicit negative of cinema: "Avoid cinematic lighting, dramatic color grading, or stylized composition"; "grounded photographic look (natural lighting, believable detail, no cinematic grading)"; "not an overly enhanced or cinematic movie-poster image"; "The image should feel honest and unposed, with real skin texture" [21][22]. Realism is built from imperfection: "real texture (pores, wrinkles, fabric wear, imperfections)" and avoiding "studio polish" and "heavy retouching" [22].
- **Film.** "Shot like a 35mm film photograph", "subtle film grain" [21][22]. **No film stock brand names** (Kodak, Portra, Ektachrome, Fuji, Cinestill) in either OpenAI guide [21][22].
- **Emotion, expression, body.** The guides prompt emotion through concrete expression and action, not mood adjectives: "kind expression, gentle eyes, and a brave but warm demeanor"; "She is clearly afraid but focused on escaping"; "looking down at the open book, not at the camera"; "hands naturally gripping the handlebars"; "He is calmly adjusting a net"; "kneels beside", "offering reassurance" [21][22].
- **Model facts.** The API guide lists gpt-image-2, gpt-image-1.5, gpt-image-1 and gpt-image-1-mini; for gpt-image-2 input fidelity is not adjustable ("the model processes every image input at high fidelity automatically"); the API page itself gives almost no style guidance [23].

### B2. VENDOR: what Google documents [24][25][26][27][28][29]
- **Describe, don't list.** "Describe the scene, don't just list keywords" [27]; the photorealistic template: "A photorealistic [shot type] of [subject], [action or expression], set in [environment]. The scene is illuminated by [lighting description], creating a [mood] atmosphere. Captured with a [camera/lens details], emphasizing [key textures and details]" [27]; near-identical template on ai.google.dev: "[Description of the light]. Shot from a [camera angle] with a [lens type]" [26]. Note that **[mood] is a documented slot** in Google's template, and **[action or expression]** sits beside the subject [27].
- **Lighting and colour vocabulary.** "three-point softbox setup", "Chiaroscuro lighting with harsh, high contrast", "Golden hour backlighting creating long shadows", "Cinematic color grading with muted teal tones", "high saturation", "Pronounced grain", "warm studio background", "deep cherry red studio backdrop" [24]; "Golden hour light, soft diffused illumination", moods "Serene, dramatic, somber", colour palettes stated explicitly ("vibrant, deep blues and bright yellows") [27]; "Make the lighting warmer" and "Change the character's expression to be more serious" as iteration steps [28]. Imagen's own modifier list: lighting "natural, dramatic, warm, cold"; camera settings "motion blur, soft focus, bokeh, portrait"; lenses "35mm, 50mm, fisheye, wide angle, macro"; film types "black and white, polaroid" [29].
- **Film and cameras.** Google names camera brands and formats but not stocks: "shot on medium-format analog film, pronounced grain", "as if on 1980s color film, slightly grainy", "Fujifilm camera for authentic color science", "cheap disposable camera for a raw, nostalgic flash aesthetic", "GoPro" [24]. Imagen names "polaroid" as a film type [29]. **No Portra, Ektachrome, Superia or Cinestill** in any Google page fetched [24][25][26][27][28][29].
- **Registers.** "Fashion magazine style editorial", "Cinematic lighting effect", "Cinematic color grading" appear in the Nano Banana guide [24]; "film noir", "1990s product photography", "photorealistic" as style descriptors [25]. **"Documentary" does not appear** as a documented register in any Google page fetched; it shows up only in third-party prompt guides (community).
- **Expression and body language.** The Gemini 2.5 Flash guide tells you to specify "Warm smiles, knowing expressions, specific postures and gestures" [27]; "Posing with a confident, statuesque stance, slightly turned" [24].
- **Negatives.** "Instead of saying 'no cars', describe the scene positively by saying, 'an empty, deserted street with no signs of traffic'" [27][28].
- **Kelvin and complementary schemes.** Nothing solid found. No Google or OpenAI page fetched uses a Kelvin value, and none uses "complementary" as a colour-scheme instruction; Google's closest is a "[color scheme]" slot in its design template and "four different color palettes" as an iteration request [30].

### B3. PRACTITIONER: what a working photographer found [31]
Dmitri at analog.cafe (film-photography site, July 2024) reviewed AI film-look tutorials. On Tao Prompts' Midjourney V6 examples: prompts ended "Arri Alexa film", "Shot on Kodak Portra film", "CineStill film", "Shot on Agfa Vista film", "Shot on Kodak Tri X film", "Shot on Kodak Portra 160 film"; "Curiously, using those endings did not make any difference; Tao admits it in his videos and yet uses them anyway. They all produced the same generic look with soft colours and identical textures" [31]. His conclusion: "I doubt that machine learning models will ever accurately replicate specific film looks" [31]. Caveat: this is Midjourney V6, not GPT Image or Gemini; no equivalent practitioner test on the two vendors' models was found. Nothing solid found from PetaPixel, Fstoppers or DPReview.

### B4. COMMUNITY: the film-stock lore [32]
Midlibrary's "Kodak Portra 400" Midjourney style page describes "warm tones and soft pastels, with gentle gradients and muted greens", benchmarked across "16 standardized prompts", but states its images "are not representative of real artists' works or real-world prototypes" [32]. Dozens of listicle pages (search results only; not fetched as evidence) claim "shot on Kodak Portra 400" beats "photorealistic"; treat as lore. Verdict: **film-stock names are community lore, not vendor-documented, and the one practitioner test found says they do nothing on Midjourney V6.** The vendor-safe equivalents are "35mm film photograph, subtle film grain" [21], "medium-format analog film, pronounced grain" [24], "1980s color film, slightly grainy" [24].

## Proposed clause phrasings (concrete, each with rationale + source #)
Each clause is written to be dropped into the scene → subject → details → constraints order OpenAI recommends [21] and the Google template slots [27].

1. **Grounded register (constraint clause).** "Natural available light, realistic colours, no cinematic grading, no dramatic colour grading; honest and unposed, with real skin texture." Rationale: this is OpenAI's own definition of the documentary look, stated as a negative of cinema [21][22]; Pollack's "restrained, and all the more powerful for it" [18].

2. **Editorial register (style clause).** "Fashion-magazine editorial, shot on medium-format analog film, pronounced grain, controlled studio light." Rationale: verbatim Google style tail [24]; keeps "editorial" tied to a documented phrase rather than a bare adjective.

3. **Cinematic register (style clause).** "Cinematic colour grading with muted teal shadows and warm skin, shallow depth of field (f/1.8)." Rationale: Google documents "Cinematic color grading with muted teal tones" and the f-number style [24][25]; OpenAI shows this register is a default the grounded clause must explicitly switch off [21].

4. **Single accent colour (colour clause).** "A muted, low-saturation palette, greys and browns, with one accent: a [red umbrella / yellow taxi / red ceiling] as the only saturated colour." Rationale: Leiter's "subdued chromatic accents" [9] and Eggleston's red as a whole surface [8]; vendors steer colour by naming the coloured object [24][25], so the accent is a noun, not "vibrant".

5. **Temperature as sources, not Kelvin (light clause).** "Lit only by a warm sodium streetlamp from the left and the cold blue glow of a television through the window." Rationale: no vendor page uses Kelvin (absence in [21][22][24][26][27][29]); Google asks for one light source and its direction in template form [26][27]; Hido's TV blue and halogen warmth are documented narrative colours [10][12][14].

6. **The lit window (scene clause).** "A suburban house at night, one window lit, curtains half drawn, a car in the drive; no people visible." Rationale: the three signifiers named in the Hido gallery text [14] and Hido's own "you imagine the stories of the people inside" [10]; "no people visible" is stated positively as the model docs prefer [27][28].

7. **Between-moment (moment clause).** "The moment just after something has happened: nobody mid-action, posture slack, one person looking at another, not at the camera." Rationale: Hido's "moment right before ... or just after" [10][11], Cook on Frank's "pose between moments" [6], Berger's parting look "not directed towards the viewer" [2], OpenAI's "not at the camera" example [21].

8. **Gesture tethered to texture (subject clause).** "Her hand rests on the dog's rough coat, thumb moving through the fur; her face is turned away toward the window." Rationale: Berger's rule that isolated gestures go mute and must "contain an idea", here touch [2]; Barthes' hand "at the right degree of openness" [1]; OpenAI accepts hand-action phrasing ("hands naturally gripping") [21]. Covers the face-out-of-frame case by giving the hand the emotional job (INFERENCE from [1][2][21]).

9. **Unplanted tender prop (details clause).** "On the table: a chipped mug, a folded letter, a child's plaster on the man's knuckle." Rationale: Barthes' punctum is a small partial object that must not read as intended [1]; state it as inventory with no feeling word, because "what I can name cannot really prick me" [1].

10. **Blind field (details clause).** "A second coat on the hook and a second cup, for someone who is not in the room." Rationale: the punctum "takes the spectator outside its frame" and creates a blind field [1]; the pornographic shop-window failure mode is one illuminated thing and nothing untimely [1].

11. **Isolation portrait (framing clause).** "One person, full body, small in a wide frame, holding still and looking straight into the lens, large-format stillness." Rationale: Soth's slowness and awkward silence [15], the "failure to be alone" [15]; Barthes' Look that is "held back by something interior" [1].

12. **Domestic proximity (scene + camera clause).** "Inside the family kitchen, on-camera flash or a single lamp, taken by someone who lives here; subjects ignore the camera." Rationale: Goldin's "this is my party. This is my family" [16], Sultan's "boundaries blur, my distance slips" [17]; Google's "cheap disposable camera for a raw, nostalgic flash aesthetic" is the documented lever for the snapshot look [24].

13. **Time's melt (details clause).** "Worn edges: a faded print on the wall, a dated haircut, a cracked vinyl chair." Rationale: Sontag's pathos by virtue of being photographed and "time's relentless melt" [3]; Barthes' "dated fashion" that touches him [1]; OpenAI's "fabric wear, imperfections" as realism [22].

14. **Mood slot, kept short (atmosphere clause).** "Creating a somber, quiet atmosphere." Rationale: Google documents [mood] as a template slot with "serene, dramatic, somber" [27]; keep one word, and let clauses 4 to 13 carry the feeling, since the photographers agree that a labelled feeling is the studium, not the punctum [1][2].

What not to write (and why): "vibrant" as a global adjective (no vendor uses it alone; it fights the single-accent rule [9][24]); "shot on Kodak Portra 400" (undocumented by both vendors; the one practitioner test found no effect [31]); "5600K daylight" (no vendor page uses Kelvin); "a poignant detail" or "an emotional photograph" (Barthes: naming it disarms it [1]); a list of feelings with no object for them (Berger: isolated expression is "mute or caricatural" [2]).
