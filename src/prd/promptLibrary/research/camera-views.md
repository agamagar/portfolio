# Camera views: research dossier
_Researched 2026-09-03. Sources listed at the bottom, numbered; cite by number inline._

Scope: the vocabulary photographers, cinematographers, product photographers, illustrators and 3D artists use for WHERE THE CAMERA IS relative to a subject, so that a prompt can re-render a reference subject from a different camera position. Everything not marked "(inference)" or UNVERIFIED was read on the cited page.

## What the medium actually is

A camera position is not one thing; it is a bundle of independent axes, and every craft names a different subset of them. Untangling the axes is the whole job, because most prompt failures come from a term that silently mixes two of them ("45-degree" is the worst offender: it can mean an orbit angle or an elevation angle, see below).

**Axis (a): camera HEIGHT / vertical angle relative to the subject.** Cinematography names this ladder from top to bottom: bird's-eye, high angle, neutral or eye-level, low angle, worm's-eye [32]. Definitions:

- Bird's-eye view: "an elevated view of an object or location from a very steep viewing angle, creating a perspective as if the observer were a bird in flight looking downward" [7]. Note it is STEEP, not necessarily vertical: Bing Maps' "Bird's eye view" imagery is "angled at 40 degrees rather than being straight down" [7]. In film, "taken directly above the scene to establish the landscape and the actors relationship to it" [3].
- Overhead shot: StudioBinder defines it as "from above, looking down on your subject" at "90 degrees above" [1]; Wikipedia says the term is "fairly synonymous with bird's-eye view but tends to imply a vantage point of a lesser height" [7]. So "overhead" is itself ambiguous between the two sources: exactly vertical in film glossaries, merely elevated in general usage. Prefer "directly overhead, camera pointing straight down" when you mean 90 degrees.
- Aerial shot: "captured from way up high" via helicopter or drone, to establish "a large expanse of scenery" [1]; aerial view "can refer to any view from a great height, even at a wide angle" [7].
- High angle: "the camera looks down on the character or subject from an elevated perspective. This can range from just slightly above the subject to nearly overhead" [35a]; "the camera points down at your subject" [1]; makes subjects look "weaker and less powerful" [33]. The difference from bird's-eye is height: a high angle "requires only modest elevation" while a bird's-eye "necessitates significant height" [10]. No degree range is quoted by any source read [1][3][10][35a].
- Eye level: "the camera is level or looking straight on with the subject" [3]; "little to no psychological effect on the viewer" [3]; "a neutral perspective (not superior or inferior)" [1]; "shows subjects as equals" [32].
- Low angle: "positioned low on the vertical axis, anywhere below the eye line, looking up" [9]; "frames the subject from below their eyeline" [1]; "makes people (and things) look bigger, more courageous and more important" [33]. No degree range quoted [1][9][35b].
- Worm's-eye view: "also known as a frog's-eye view, is a description of the view of a scene from a very steep, low angle", "looking up from just above the ground or low on the vertical axis", drawn in "three-point perspective" with vanishing points top, left and right; "objects appear larger than they are" [8]. Film usage: "looks up from the ground... the view that a child or a pet would have" [3].
- Ground level: "when your camera's height is on ground level with your subject" [1]. Distinct from worm's-eye: ground level can be a level lens axis at floor height; worm's-eye is tilted steeply up [1][8] (the distinction is stated separately in each source; combining them is inference).

**Axis (f): height quoted against a human body.** Cinematography gives a second ladder, in body landmarks rather than adjectives: overhead, eye level, shoulder level, hip level (a.k.a. cowboy), knee level, ground level [1]. StudioBinder's glosses: shoulder level is "as high as your subject's shoulders" and lets "the actor's head reach the top of the frame"; hip level is "roughly waist-high", useful when subjects differ in height or for action "near the hip, like weapons being drawn"; knee level is at the subject's knees and reads as "superiority" or is used for "characters walking"; ground level is at ground height, showing walking "without revealing their face" [1]. These are HEIGHTS, not tilts: a knee-level camera can look level (a low viewpoint) or tilt up (a low angle); the sources do not separate the two, so treat "knee level, looking slightly up" as the safe phrasing (inference).

**Axis (b): horizontal ORBIT around the subject (azimuth).** Cinematography: "The classic angles are front, three-quarter, profile, three-quarter rear and rear" [32]. Film-language glosses: "A head-on frontal view makes us feel really engaged with the subject"; "With a three-quarter shot we're a bit less involved"; "In a side view it's more as if we're just watching them as an observer"; "A back view can mean several things: we're seeing them from the view of a watcher; they are ignoring another character; or they are upset and vulnerable" [33]. Profile shot: "frames the subject from the side, showing their face in full profile, typically with one eye visible and the rest of the features in clean outline" [34]. Illustration uses the same orbit for turnaround sheets: "A turn or turnaround must include at least a front view of the character, a rear view, a profile, and a three-quarter view" [36]; the three-quarter "blends the front and side views, providing a more natural sense of depth and form" [37]. Portraiture quotes the number: in a three-quarter profile "the subject's head is turned away from the viewer by roughly 30 to 45 degrees, revealing more of one side of the face than the other" [38]. Ideogram's vendor glossary lists front view, side profile, back view, three-quarter view ("angled between front and side"), behind-the-subject [41].

**Axis (c): DISTANCE / shot size.** The film ladder, with where the frame cuts a standing body [2]: extreme wide (subject "small against their location"), wide/long (entire subject in frame with substantial space), full (head to toe, minimal space), medium wide ("from roughly the knees up"), cowboy ("from roughly mid-thighs up"), medium ("from roughly the waist up"), medium close-up ("from roughly the chest up"), close-up ("fills your frame with a part of your subject", typically the face), extreme close-up (frame filled so closely that "tiny details... would otherwise be difficult to see", often macro lenses) [2]. Wikipedia's shorter ladder: long/wide "shows the environment around the subjects", full "the entirety of the subject is just visible within the frame", medium "frame stops either just above or just below the waist", close-up "shoulder line is visible", plus the Italian shot where "only a person's eyes are visible" [4]. For objects, product photography swaps the body cuts for "macro" ("extreme close-up capturing fine details", "supplements other angles rather than replacing them") [25] and Imagen's own vocabulary uses "close-up" vs "zoomed out / taken from far away" [20].

**Axis (d): camera ROLL.** Dutch angle: "the camera is tilted so that vertical lines appear at an angle to the frame, or the horizon is not parallel with the frame's bottom"; synonyms "Dutch tilt, canted angle, vortex plane, oblique angle, and Durkin"; effect "a sense of unease or disorientation"; "a viewpoint akin to tilting one's head to the side" [5]. StudioBinder: "tilting the camera so that the horizon line is slanted", "the tilt can be subtle or extreme" [35c]. Product photographers borrow it as "Dutch angle (Dutch tilt)": "camera tilted on its x-axis creating off-center horizons" [25]. No source quotes a degree range [5][25][35c].

**Axis (e): LENS focal length, and what it does to the same viewpoint.** Angle of view falls as focal length rises: on full frame, 24mm covers 84.1 degrees diagonal, 35mm 63.4, 50mm 46.8, 85mm 28.6, 135mm about 18; "normal" lenses are "36 to 60 mm in 35 mm film format" [12]. The perspective effect, though, is a matter of distance, not glass: "linear perspective changes are caused by distance, not by the lens per se, two shots of the same scene from the same distance will exhibit identical perspective geometry, regardless of lens used" [11]. Extension (wide-angle) distortion: shot close with a wide lens, "objects near the lens appear abnormally large relative to distant objects"; compression (telephoto) distortion: shot from far with a long lens, "distant objects appear similar in size to each other" and depth flattens [11]. "Lenses with focal lengths from about 85 through 135 mm are generally considered to be good portrait lenses" [11]. Cinematography's shorthand: "wide-angle lenses also seem to exaggerate depth" and telephoto reduces apparent distance between objects [32]. So in a prompt, focal length is a proxy for camera DISTANCE at a fixed framing; say both (inference from [11]).

**The 3D-artist axis: projection.** Game and DCC cameras add a switch photographers do not have. Perspective: "Objects on the screen becomes smaller when they are far away"; orthogonal/orthographic: "Objects remain the same size on the screen no matter how far away they are" [40]; Unity: perspective renders "with perspective intact", orthographic "uniformly, with no sense of perspective", and Field of View is "the Camera's view angle, measured in degrees" [39]. Orthographic projection is the drafting default: "all the projection lines are orthogonal to the projection plane", so measurements can be taken directly [14]. Isometric is a named orthographic viewpoint: "35.264 degrees elevation combined with a 45 degree rotation about the vertical axis", axes 120 degrees apart; most games use a 2:1 dimetric approximation at about 26.565 degrees to horizontal [13]. Ideogram glosses it as "angled top-down with parallel lines and no distortion" [41]. 3D camera-control research parameterises the whole bundle as five numbers: "azimuth, elevation, and radius define the camera position in spherical coordinates, while pitch and yaw define the relative camera rotation" [22]; multi-view benchmarks use azimuth plus elevation [23].

**Drafting terms that collide with photography.** Plan: "a view of a 3-dimensional object seen from vertically above (or sometimes below)"; elevation: "the view of a 3-dimensional object from the position of a vertical plane beside an object" (front, back, left, right); section: a view "from the position of a plane through the object" [6]. So "elevation" in drafting is a horizontal-axis orthographic SIDE view, while "elevation" in 3D camera parameters is the vertical ANGLE of the camera above the ground plane [6][22]. Never write "elevation" bare.

## What separates a convincing result from a generic one

1. **Name the axis, never the number alone.** Text-to-image models "struggle to follow even simple geometric instructions such as 'back view', '30 degree left-side view', or '45 degree top-down perspective'" and "hallucinate incorrect poses, collapse to biased canonical angles, or produce inconsistent geometry across trials" [22]. Multi-view models are "sensitive to the input azimuth angles and none of the methods are robust to higher elevation angles" [23]. A bare "45 degrees" is the least reliable token you can write; a named view plus a description of what becomes visible is the most (inference from [22][23] plus the vendor guides' preference for named terms [15][18]).

2. **Describe the geometric consequence, not just the label.** The craft sources define each angle by what it does to the picture: worm's-eye uses "three-point perspective" with vanishing points "top, left, and right" and "objects appear larger than they are" [8]; a flat lay tilted to 80 degrees shows "visible perspective distortion" at the frame edges [30]; a high angle makes the "point of focus often get swallowed up" [10]. Putting the consequence in the clause ("the top face visible, verticals converging downward") gives the model a second, redundant cue (inference).

3. **Treat focal length as distance.** Since perspective is set by distance and not by the lens [11], a prompt that says "85mm" without saying "from across the room" is under-specified. Pair them ("shot from a distance on a long lens so the depth compresses") and the model has both the look and the geometry to hit (inference from [11]).

4. **Eye level for an object is a height, not an eye.** Product photographers put the camera "directly in line with the product" [24], at the "item's center point", "without tilting your camera" [28], "on the same plane and at the same height" [26]. Say that; do not say "eye level" and hope.

5. **The standard product set is small and well agreed.** Practical Ecommerce's six: front, profile, 45-degree ("also called the three-quarter angle"), back, top ("often called the birds-eye-view"), macro [29]. Saturday School's set: straight-on (0 degrees), angled (25 to 75 degrees), hero (below 0 degrees, "camera lower than subject"), flat lay (90 degrees), Dutch, macro [25]. Kalory adds the purposes: eye-level for "ecommerce, electronics, packaging"; high angle for "food, cosmetics, flat lays, multi-product"; low angle for "watches, cars, premium goods"; bird's-eye for "kits, sets, collections" [24]. The hero angle is a LOW angle in every product source read [25][27]; one search snippet quoted "typically around -15 degrees" but the page (Pro Photo Studio) returned 403 on every fetch, so that number is UNVERIFIED.

6. **Consistency needs a repeated preserve-list, per the vendors.** OpenAI: "State exclusions and invariants explicitly" and "repeat the preserve list on each iteration to reduce drift" [16]; its example constraint block for an edit reads "Preserve camera angle, room lighting, floor shadows, and surrounding objects" and "Keep all other aspects of the image unchanged" [15]. Google: "Keep everything else in the image exactly the same" and "preserving the original style, lighting, and composition" [18]. When the CAMERA is the thing changing, the preserve-list must drop "camera angle" and keep geometry, materials, labels and lighting instead (inference).

## Model-side levers

**OpenAI (gpt-image family).** The cookbook guide's composition rule: "Specify framing and viewpoint (close-up, wide, top-down), perspective/angle (eye-level, low-angle)" plus lighting, and "If layout matters, call out placement" [15]. Its worked prompt: "medium close-up at eye level, using a 50mm lens. Soft coastal daylight, shallow depth of field" [15][16]. On realism: "camera/composition terms (lens, aperture feel, lighting) often steer realism more reliably than generic '8K/ultra-detailed'" [15][16]. Prompt order: "background/scene, subject, key details, constraints", with "short labeled segments or line breaks instead of one long paragraph" [16]. Preservation language it models: "Do not change her face, facial features, skin tone, body shape, pose, or identity in any way. Preserve her exact likeness, expression, hairstyle, and proportions"; "Preserve product geometry and label legibility exactly"; "Preserve the exact layout, proportions, and perspective"; "Do not change anything else" [15][16]. For multi-image composites it asks to match "lighting, perspective, scale, and shadows so the composite looks naturally captured in the original photo" [16]. API side: gpt-image-2 "processes every image input at high fidelity automatically", so input_fidelity is omitted for it; models listed are gpt-image-2, gpt-image-1.5, gpt-image-1, gpt-image-1-mini; the guide warns the model "may have difficulty placing elements precisely in structured or layout-sensitive compositions" [17]. Neither OpenAI page gives a worked "same subject, new angle" prompt; the closest is the interior example that holds "camera angle" fixed while changing an object [15]. So the reverse (hold subject, change camera) is our own construction: nothing solid found in OpenAI docs for that exact case.

**Google (Gemini image / Nano Banana, Imagen).** Core rule: "Describe the scene, don't just list keywords" [18]. Camera rule: "Control the camera: Use photographic and cinematic language", with the term list "wide-angle shot, macro shot, low-angle perspective, 85mm portrait lens, Dutch angle" [18]. Template: "A photorealistic [type of shot] of a [subject description] in a [setting description]. [Description of the light]. Shot from a [camera angle] with a [lens type]" [19]; worked examples use "low perspective", "slightly elevated 45-degree shot", "wide-angle lens" [19]. Edit template: "Using the provided image of [subject], please [add/remove/modify] [element] to/from the scene. Ensure the change is [description of how the change should integrate]" with "Keep everything else in the image exactly the same" [18][19]. Gemini 3 image models accept "up to 14 reference images" including "up to 10 images of objects with high-fidelity" for consistency [19]. Imagen's older prompt guide gives camera as three modifier lists: proximity "close up, taken from far away"; position "aerial, from below"; lens "35mm, 50mm, fisheye, wide angle, macro" [20]. A Google Cloud "Gemini image generation best practices" page appeared in search with glosses for eye-level, low-angle, high-angle, bird's-eye and worm's-eye; the page rendered as navigation only on three fetch attempts, so those glosses are UNVERIFIED and not cited here.

**Ideogram.** Its prompting reference is the only vendor page read that separates scene-level from subject-level perspective, and it lists front view, side profile, back view, three-quarter view, low angle, high angle, eye-level, overhead angle, underside view, isometric view, Dutch angle, over-the-shoulder and POV as accepted tokens [41]. Useful as a cross-vendor vocabulary check even if we are not targeting Ideogram.

**Evidence on what models actually obey.**
- The GPT-4o empirical study tested camera control as bokeh, focal length, shutter speed and colour temperature: GPT-4o showed "decent results in controlling bokeh blur parameters and color temperature" but "still falls short in adjusting focal length and shutter speed", sometimes "leading to inconsistent visual semantics or incorrect visual effects"; "Gemini 2.0 Flash struggles significantly across all camera control scenarios" [21]. Its novel-view-synthesis task (image-to-3D group) is scored only as a binary, with GPT-4o and Gemini 2.0 Flash both marked success and no identity-consistency analysis [21]. Note the study is of the spring-2025 models; it says nothing about gpt-image-1.5, gpt-image-2 or Gemini 2.5/3 image, so treat it as a floor, not a current reading.
- Viewpoint-token research (2026) states the baseline problem in prompt terms: models fail "back view", "30 degree left-side view", "45 degree top-down perspective", "collapse to biased canonical angles", and a prior camera-control method "overfits to lions, ostriches, shoes, sofas, and teddy bears 94.2% of the time" on novel objects [22]. Their fix is learned tokens, not better prose, which is itself evidence that prose alone under-controls precise angles (inference).
- MVGBench (single image to multi-view): "none of the methods are robust to higher elevation angles" and "no method can achieve the best performance in both" 3D consistency and image quality [23]. Expect the top-down and high-elevation re-renders of a reference subject to be the weakest (inference).
- Practical reading (inference): shot size, named vertical angle (eye/low/high/top-down), Dutch roll and lens "look" (bokeh, wide vs long) are the levers the vendors document and the benchmarks partly confirm; exact degrees, back views and steep elevations are the unreliable ones. Compensate with named views, geometric consequences in words, and a reference image for identity.

## Proposed clause upgrades

Each clause is written to be dropped after a subject description and before a lighting/material block, in the vendors' "shot, then angle, then lens" order [15][19]. Glosses in brackets are for the library, not the prompt.

1. **Front, eye level (packshot front).** "A straight-on front view at eye level: the camera directly in front of the subject on the same plane and at its own height, lens level with no tilt, so the front face is seen square and its sides are hidden." Rationale: product default, "same plane and at the same height" [26], "directly in line with the product" [24], "eye-level" is a vendor keyword [15][18]. Sources 15, 24, 26.

2. **Three-quarter front, eye level.** "A three-quarter front view at eye level, the camera rotated about 45 degrees around the subject so the front and one side are both visible and the near corner leads, lens level." Rationale: "also called the three-quarter angle", "shows multiple sides of a product" [29]; three-quarter "blends the front and side views" [37]; the number is stated with its axis (around the subject) because bare degrees under-control [22]. Sources 22, 29, 37.

3. **Profile / side view.** "A pure side view in profile, the camera at 90 degrees to the subject's facing, lens level at the subject's height, so only the silhouette and one side plane are seen, flat and outline-led." Rationale: profile "showing their face in full profile... in clean outline" [34]; "flat, useful for formal effects" [32]. Sources 32, 34.

4. **Three-quarter rear.** "A three-quarter rear view, the camera behind and to one side of the subject at eye level, so the back and one side are visible and the front is hidden." Rationale: "three-quarter rear" is one of the five classic lateral angles [32]; rear views "feel closed off" [32]; back views are the known weak spot, so the visible-faces gloss is redundancy [22]. Sources 22, 32.

5. **Rear / back view.** "A direct back view at eye level, the camera squarely behind the subject, lens level, showing the rear face only." Rationale: turnaround "rear view" [36]; product "back angle... can reveal important details" [29]; benchmarks name "back view" as a failure mode, so say "rear face only" [22]. Sources 22, 29, 36.

6. **Elevated 45-degree (the product "angled" shot).** "A slightly elevated 45-degree shot: the camera raised above the subject and tilted down about 45 degrees while facing it, so the top face and the front face are both visible with the front still dominant." Rationale: Google's own example wording "slightly elevated 45-degree shot" [19]; product "angled shot" between frontal and overhead, 25 to 75 degrees [25][27]; "a bit higher than the subject" [26]. Sources 19, 25, 26, 27.

7. **High angle.** "A high-angle shot, the camera well above the subject looking down at it, verticals converging toward the ground, the subject reading smaller and the surface around it filling the frame." Rationale: "from slightly above the subject to nearly overhead" [35a]; "make people or things look weaker and less powerful" [33]; high angle for "layouts, multiple items, or surface details" [24]. Sources 24, 33, 35a.

8. **Top-down, directly overhead (photographic).** "A top-down shot from directly overhead, the lens pointing straight down at 90 degrees, so only the top face is visible and nothing of the sides, with perspective kept true across the frame." Rationale: "top-down" is an OpenAI keyword [15]; overhead "90 degrees above" [1]; flat lay's rule that anything short of 90 shows edge distortion [30]. Sources 1, 15, 30.

9. **Flat lay.** "A flat lay: the subject laid on a flat surface and photographed from directly above at exactly 90 degrees on a 35 to 50mm lens, every element on one plane, no perspective skew at the edges." Rationale: definition and "35mm and 50mm" lens advice [30]; product use "consistency... apparel and handheld items" [27]. Sources 27, 30.

10. **Plan view (orthographic, drawn).** "An orthographic plan view: seen from vertically above with parallel projection, no perspective and no foreshortening with distance, as in a technical drawing." Rationale: plan "seen from vertically above" [6]; orthographic "objects remain the same size on the screen no matter how far away they are" [40]. Sources 6, 40.

11. **Low-angle hero.** "A low-angle hero shot, the camera slightly below the subject's base and tilted up, so the subject towers, its underside edge shows and the ground plane recedes behind it." Rationale: hero "makes your subject look bigger, taller, and more dominant", "even slight angles below product level create impact" [25]; hero shots "present products as grand and premium" [27]; low-angle is a vendor keyword [15][18]. Sources 15, 18, 25, 27.

12. **Worm's-eye / ground level.** "A worm's-eye view from ground level, the camera on the ground tilted steeply upward, three-point perspective with verticals converging toward the top of the frame and the subject looming." Rationale: "very steep, low angle... looking up from just above the ground", "three-point perspective" [8]; ground level "on ground level with your subject" [1]; Imagen's "from below" [20]. Sources 1, 8, 20.

13. **Body-height ladder (people or human-scale subjects).** "Camera at knee height, lens level with a slight upward tilt (knee-level shot)" / "camera at hip height, lens level (hip-level or cowboy shot)" / "camera at shoulder height, lens level so the head reaches the top of the frame (shoulder-level shot)." Rationale: StudioBinder's height ladder and glosses [1]; heights are given with tilt stated separately because the sources fuse them (inference). Source 1.

14. **Dutch angle.** "A Dutch angle: the camera rolled about its lens axis so the horizon and all verticals lean, the tilt clear but not extreme, giving a canted, unsettled frame." Rationale: "vertical lines appear at an angle to the frame" [5]; "the tilt can be subtle or extreme" [35c]; "Dutch angle" is on Google's camera-term list [18]. Sources 5, 18, 35c.

15. **Isometric.** "An isometric view: orthographic projection with no perspective, the camera turned 45 degrees around the subject and raised 35 degrees, so the top and two sides are visible with all parallel edges staying parallel." Rationale: 45 degrees about the vertical axis, 35.264 degrees elevation, axes at 120 degrees [13]; Ideogram gloss "angled top-down with parallel lines and no distortion" [41]. Sources 13, 41.

16. **Wide-angle close vs telephoto far (lens pair for the same viewpoint).** "Shot from close on a wide-angle lens (around 24mm), so the near edges swell, depth stretches and the background falls away small" versus "shot from a distance on a long lens (around 135mm), so depth compresses, the subject's faces stay in proportion and the background is brought up close." Rationale: extension vs compression distortion and the rule that distance, not the lens, sets the geometry [11]; angle-of-view figures [12]; camera terms "steer realism more reliably" [15]. Sources 11, 12, 15.

17. **Macro / extreme close-up.** "An extreme close-up on a macro lens, the frame filled with one detail of the subject so the surface texture reads and nothing of the whole silhouette is in frame." Rationale: ECU "fills the frame... tiny details", "often shot with macro lenses" [2]; product macro "emphasize texture and minute details" [25]; "macro shot" is a Google term [18]. Sources 2, 18, 25.

18. **Extreme wide / establishing.** "An extreme wide shot, the subject small against its whole location, the camera far back at eye level so the setting dominates." Rationale: EWS "small against their location" [2]; Imagen "taken from far away / zoomed out" [20]. Sources 2, 20.

**Consistency clause (hold the subject, move the camera).** Proposed: "Using the provided image of [subject] as the reference, re-render the same subject from [named view clause]. Preserve the subject's exact geometry, proportions, materials, colours, surface details and label legibility; keep the same lighting direction and background treatment. Change only the camera position and what that new position reveals. Do not change anything else." Rationale: assembled from Google's edit template "Using the provided image of [subject]..." and "Keep everything else in the image exactly the same" [18][19], and OpenAI's invariant style "Preserve product geometry and label legibility exactly", "Do not change anything else", plus its instruction to "repeat the preserve list on each iteration to reduce drift" [15][16]. The "keep the same lighting direction" line is our addition: neither vendor shows a camera-move example, and the geometric truth is that a moved camera changes the visible shading even under fixed lights, so this is a soft hold (inference). When the target is gpt-image-2 the reference is read at high fidelity by default [17]; for Gemini, supply the subject as one of the reference images [19].
