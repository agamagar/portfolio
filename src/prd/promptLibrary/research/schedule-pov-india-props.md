# Schedule POV, Indian props by moment: research dossier
_Researched 2026-09-15. Sources listed at the bottom, numbered; cite by number inline._

Scope: six stills seen from the person's own eyes (their hands entering from the bottom edge, a phone in hand or a task under way) in a metro Indian city, for a grocery app's scheduled-delivery feature. The designer's brief: some Indian context, not overdone. This file answers one question per moment: which two or three real objects would actually be in that field of view, what they are called, and which of them are cliche flags.

Conventions. [n] is a source in this file. [MC n] is source n in the existing material-culture dossier (research/schedule-images-material-culture.md); [CP n] is source n in the colour-photography dossier (research/schedule-images-colour-photography.md). INFERENCE is a reasonable reading of a sourced fact; UNVERIFIED is a widely believed claim for which no page was fetched that confirms it. Where a scene came back empty the text says "nothing solid found". Dashes inside quoted product copy have been replaced with commas or the word "to" (house rule); nothing else was altered.

## The register, briefly (what the existing dossier already settles)

The existing dossier [MC] settles the register and this file does not restate it. In short: hard, wipeable, durable surfaces (steel, moulded plastic, distemper, mosaic or kota floors) accumulated over decades, brand names used as common nouns (Godrej, Nilkamal, Milton, Hawkins), and old canon layered under new (a 1990s almirah, a 2020s phone, a 2024 paper bag) rather than a matched set [MC 1, 2, 12, 13, 15]. The commute is colour-coded by city (purple-and-white Mumbai local with a yellow front and open doors; pink Ladies Only seats in the Delhi Metro; black-and-yellow Mumbai auto against green-and-yellow Delhi CNG auto; red BEST, green or red DTC) [MC 22, 28, 30, 33, 34]. The kirana front has a metal shutter, jute sacks, sachet strings and a ledger [MC 36], and the delivery object is a printed brown paper bag with a two-colour illustration, not a plastic tote [MC 38, 39].

The colour dossier [CP] settles what to refuse: the "genda phool effect" (marigold flower market, Varanasi, grain-heavy film) [CP 28], the Instagram-India list (fishing nets, train windows, looms, water pots, "narrow bazaars in golden light", weathered doorways) [CP 39], the saturated "exoticness and poverty" extremes [CP 21], and the grey-polluted-chaos alternative [CP 26].

What the first-person viewpoint changes, and why this file exists: the POV throws most of the canon out of frame. Lying on the back, the almirah, the floor and the window grill are gone and the ceiling fan is the whole picture. In an auto, the black-and-yellow shell that makes the postcard is behind the eye; what is in front is the fare meter and the driver's back. At the counter it is the cooker and the granite, not the kitchen. In the jam it is a countdown timer and the pillion's view over a helmet. INFERENCE: the first-person frame is itself the strongest anti-cliche device available, because every cliche on the [CP] list needs distance and a wide field to work. The job of the prop clause is therefore small: name the one or two objects a hand-and-phone frame would actually contain, in the words they are called, and stop.

## Moment by moment

### 1. "Last night, heading to sleep." (lying on the back, phone held above the face)

What is really overhead in a metro bedroom is a three-blade ceiling fan on a downrod, and almost nothing else. The 1200 mm (48 inch) sweep is the retail default for a bedroom: Crompton's own 1200 mm collection filters by "Opal White", "Ivory" and "Brown" [1]; the Sea Bliss is "Ivory", 3 blades, "1200mm", "48 Inches", mounted on a downrod [2]; Orient's current range lists "Clay Brown" and "White" at 1200 mm, and wood-look "Honey Maple Wood" and "Dark Teak Wood" for the newer BLDC decorative fans [3]. From below the object reads as a round motor body on a rod with three flat arms; INFERENCE: ivory or brown blades against a whitish distemper ceiling [MC 9], slightly off-centre in the frame because the phone, not the fan, is centred. On light: with the phone as the only source the fan casts no shadow on the ceiling and at most the underside of the blades is half-lit; the "fan shadow" the brief mentions only exists when a wall or bedside lamp is on (INFERENCE, no page fetched on this).

The wall regulator is a real object but it lives on the modular switchboard by the door or bedside, not overhead: Havells sells a 1-module "Coral Step Fan Regulator" in white plastic [4, 5], a 5-step model with an "ergonomically designed directional knob" [4], and a "Reo Elegant Bold 4 Steps 2 Module Soot Black" [4]. It enters a lying-down frame only if the frame tilts to the bedside wall; INFERENCE: use it only in that variant.

Night light: the "zero watt bulb" is a real Indian noun for a small incandescent that is "commonly known as a Zero Watt Bulb or Night Bulb" and actually draws "10 Watts / 15 Watts", B22 base [6]; its LED successor is sold as a 0.5 W "night lamp" in "Blue", "Yellow", "Green", "Pink", "Red", "White" [7]. This confirms the [MC] entry that was UNVERIFIED there. From below: a small coloured glow in a wall holder at the frame edge. Which colour is commonest: UNVERIFIED.

Tube light: the fluorescent tube is now an LED batten; Philips' Slimline Ultra comes as "20W, 3.8ft (1100mm)" in "Cool Day Light, Natural White, Warm White" with an "Ultra-slim profile" [8]. INFERENCE from ubiquitous imagery: it is wall-mounted high, above the door or window, not ceiling-centred, so from the bed it is a thin pale bar at the ceiling line, off at this hour.

Mosquito net: the current metro form is the pop-up tent, "self-supporting", on a "PVC Coated steel spring frame", "50 denier, 30GSM ... polyester" mesh, in "Full Pink / Full Blue" and other colours, king size "200*200*145 cms" [9]. From inside it is a mesh dome 1.4 m above the face with the fan blurred behind it. Cliche risk is high: the draped white canopy is the tropical postcard, and even the tent net competes with the phone as subject. Recommendation: omit.

Wall calendar: Kalnirnay is the wall calendar of "1.8 crore households", where families "mark auspicious days", circle dates and record birthdays [10]; nine languages, about 19 million copies a year [11]. It is not overhead, so it is a frame-edge object only. Cliche risk is mild: the deity-printed calendar is a recognised India signal; the Kalnirnay page is a dense grid of dates, which is the honest version. Pillow edge and printed cotton bedsheet: [MC] (UNVERIFIED beyond listings). Almirah, floor, window grill: out of frame in this POV.

### 2. "On the way home. I hadn't reached yet." (from the passenger's seat)

Auto-rickshaw. The Bajaj RE seats "4 persons (with Driver)" in a body 1300 mm wide and 1700 mm high [12]; the current model has a "New digital instrument cluster with Quick start" and comes in "Eco Green", "Black Yellow" and "Golden Yellow" [13]; the driver's seat "offers genuine back support" and the controls are handlebars [14]. Piaggio's Ape is "most commonly found in the form of an autorickshaw" in India, where all production moved in 2013 [15]. The passenger's object is the fare meter, and it is a small box: a Mumbai maker's digital meter is an LCD with a "Black and White dial", "115 x 60 x 22 mm", ABS body, mounted on "dashboards or panels" [16]; another Mumbai unit has a "Separate Fare, Kilometer display", an "Integrated 'FOR HIRE' display with backlight" and "buttons on the back" [17]. INFERENCE from "dashboards": it sits on the front cowl to the right of the driver, beside the handlebar, which from the rear seat is upper-left-of-centre past the driver's shoulder. The meter is culturally loaded: the Meter Jam boycotts in Mumbai and Bengaluru protested drivers who "do not acknowledge to go by the rates of the meter" [18]. So "meter running" is an honest detail, not decoration. Display colour (green or amber backlight): UNVERIFIED.

The rain flaps are a named object: "Rikshaw Rain Parda", "Rexine", "Yellow", "Waterproof", Rs 160 a piece, made in Mumbai [19]; from inside, in rain, they turn the open side into a yellow translucent wall. A clear window in the parda: UNVERIFIED. The partition bar behind the driver's seat that passengers hold, and the driver's back with a shirt and the seat's rexine: nothing solid found on any page; mark as INFERENCE from ubiquitous imagery. The [MC] exterior facts (canvas roof, drop-down side curtains, Mumbai black-and-yellow vs Delhi green-and-yellow [MC 30]) become, from inside, a strip of painted frame at the open side and the underside of the hood.

Mumbai local: [MC 22, 23, 25] already give the pole, the FRP wood-finish seats and the open doors. From own eyes the frame is a hand on a steel pole and the door frame beyond; "train windows" are on the Instagram-India list [CP 39] and hanging at the footboard is spectacle, so keep the hand on the pole, inside. Delhi Metro: stainless coach and pink "Ladies only" seats [MC 27, 28]; from a seat, a stainless pole and the pink seat opposite; city-specific, so it reads as Delhi rather than "subway". Cab: [MC 31] gives the models and yellow plate; the driver's phone on a dashboard mount running the ride app is UNVERIFIED at page level (search summaries only), and the beaded seat cover was already "nothing solid found" in [MC]. On foot: a cloth bag is INFERENCE from [MC 36].

### 4. "Between back-to-back meetings. I didn't want to forget to order later." (a seat in a meeting room)

The one Indian-specific thing in a tech or corporate meeting room is the glass wall and its band. Indian practice is to mark glazed partitions with a "manifestation" band "at roughly eye and waist level so the glass reads as a surface"; "frosting bands or full-pane frost give a cabin privacy without losing borrowed light"; branding adds "printed logos, gradient dots, geometric patterns on the reception and meeting-room glass"; "many corporate landlords mandate it" [28]. Fit-out pages add "gradient frosting" and "aluminium or steel frames" [29]. From a seat: a frosted band at eye height that cuts the open office behind it into a top strip of ceiling and a bottom strip of chair legs.

The rest is generic office kit with an Indian tea habit. Coworking rooms list "Whiteboard", "TV/Projector", "Video facilities" [30]; Awfis includes "unlimited tea, coffee and snacks" brought by a "dedicated House-keeping person" [31], so a cup of chai on the table mid-meeting is normal, not staged. Office chai comes from a machine: Chai Point's brewing bots dispense "Chai & Filter Coffee" at "Amazon, Google, LinkedIn, Uber, WeWork" and others [32]. Paper cup: UNVERIFIED at page level (search summaries mention paper tea cups as a supply). INFERENCE: a small white paper cup, about 150 ml, beige tea. The clay kulhad is the cliche version. The conference phone: the Polycom SoundStation shipped in 1992 with "three legs" that "held microphones" and became "the triangular speakerphone that became an office icon" [33]; INFERENCE: newer Indian tech rooms have a TV with a video bar instead, so treat the Polycom as an optional prop for older corporate rooms. Lanyard: a polyester lanyard with a "PVC ID Card" is the standard "Corporate Employee ID Cards & Office Use" product, in "Blue" and other colours [34]; from own eyes it drops from the neck into the bottom of the frame beside the hands, card face turned away. Laptop and notebook: no Indian-specific source needed.

### 5. "At peak. It was going to be late in traffic anyway." (inside the jam)

The countdown timer is the strongest object here and it is sourced. Delhi has "1,018 traffic light countdown timers" beside its 1,001 signals [35]; Mumbai's honk-penalty signals reset the red "to a full 90 seconds" whenever noise passes 85 dB, so the timer "instead of dropping from ten to nine, flicked back up to ninety seconds" [36]; Bengaluru's 169 adaptive signals mirror their timers into a navigation app [37]. The hardware is a "2.5 digit" LED display in "RED & GREEN" counting "199 to 000", in a "610 x 375 x 135mm" powder-coated MS box "with 130mm hood on Top" [38]. From inside the jam: a black rectangular box beside or above the signal head with two or three large red digits counting down, green digits in the go phase.

The peak itself: TomTom's 2024 index puts Bengaluru at "34 min 10 s" per 10 km, "38%" congestion, "117 hours" lost a year, Kolkata at "34 min 33 s", Pune at "33 min 22 s" [39]; three of the world's five slowest cities are Indian, at "over 33 minutes" per 10 km [40]. The evening-peak figure for Bengaluru (45 minutes 27 seconds per 10 km) appeared only in a search summary: UNVERIFIED. Two-wheelers are "67 percent" of Delhi's registered vehicles [35], so a frame filled with scooters between the cars is statistically the truth, not a flourish.

Pillion view: nothing solid found as an essay or photo series; INFERENCE from ubiquitous imagery that the rider's helmet and shoulder fill the lower left, the road between. The helmet-law page returned 403, so the pillion helmet rule is UNVERIFIED at page level. Auto's open side: INFERENCE from [MC 30] and [19]. Tail lights: none needed. Dust and haze: [CP 26] warns that "everything was polluted everything was grey" is itself a template; a dusk haze is fine, a smog wash is the cliche. Horns are not visual; the Mumbai honk story is a text prop only.

### 6. "A birthday gift. It only had to land on the day." (wrapping at home)

None of these objects is a cliche; the risk in this moment runs the other way, toward Western party stock (balloons, confetti, ribbon curls). The stationery-shop wrap is sold by the sheet, from "₹5" a piece, in "20×28 inch" and "50x70cm" sheets, "Metallic Paper", "BOPP", "Glossy", 45 to 90 GSM, in "Birthday" and "Flower" prints [41]; the upmarket version is a 20 x 30 inch, 130 GSM matte designer sheet sold in sets [42]. From own eyes: a thin glossy printed sheet, single-side print, the paler reverse showing at the folds. Cello tape is the Indian noun: a tape "that has a transparent cellophane film as the backing", "low-tack" [43]; the desk set is an "18 mm 25 Meter Roll" at "49 microns" with a transparent dispenser, sold for "gift wrapping" [44]. The card is an Archies by default: founded 1979 in Delhi, "about 50% of India's greeting cards market", sold through "Archies Galleries" [45]; the brand name should not be legible.

The cake box is the most specific object. The industry standard is "300 to 350 GSM ITC food-grade SBS paperboard" [46]; the 1 kg box is "10x10x5" inches and the half kg "8x8x5" [46, 47]; the range is "plain white cake boxes, printed cake boxes, kraft cake boxes, handle cake boxes and window cake boxes" [47]; a window box in kraft costs Rs 10 and a white duplex half-kg box Rs 4.20 wholesale [48]; the 2 kg window box is "10x10x9inch" [49]. From own eyes: a square white or printed board box about 10 inches across, a clear film window in the lid, a tuck flap. A bakery-printed box with the shop's name and a generic "Happy Birthday": INFERENCE. Candles come as "Spiral Birthday Cake Candles with Holders", "Pack of 6", or "3mm Metallic Straight", or a metallic "Number" candle "0 to 9" [50]; the bakery adding a candle pack and a plastic knife to the bag is INFERENCE. Ribbon: polyester satin, "25mm Width", "15 Meter Length", sold for "Gift Wrapping, Decoration, Bakery & Crafts" [51].

## Proposed prop clauses

Each clause names two props, in the nouns they are called, and stops. They are written to sit after the POV and lighting clauses of the prompt, not to replace them. Rationale and sources follow each.

1. Moment 1, "Last night, heading to sleep."
   Clause: "Overhead, an ivory three-blade ceiling fan on a downrod, off-centre against a matte distemper ceiling; at the frame edge, the small blue glow of a zero-watt night bulb."

Nothing else from the [MC] bedroom canon is in this field of view. [1, 2, 3, 6, 7, MC 9]
   Variant if the frame tilts to the bedside wall: swap the night bulb for "a white modular switchboard with a step fan regulator". [4, 5]
