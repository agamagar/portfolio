# Indian colour photography: research dossier
_Researched 2026-09-04. Sources numbered at the bottom; cite by number inline._

Scope: the craft and vocabulary of Indian colour photography (documentary, street, contemporary art and commercial lifestyle), gathered so that prompts for candid Indian lifestyle stills use the terms the practitioners and their critics use. Every source below was fetched; where a fetch failed and only a search snippet survived, the claim is marked UNVERIFIED. Quotations that contained an em-dash in the original have had that dash replaced by a comma (house rule); nothing else was altered.

Coverage gaps, stated up front:
- Raghubir Singh's own essay "River of Colour: An Indian View" (1998) could not be fetched in full; his two best-known sentences from it reach us through NPR's syndicated piece [1] and a photographer's tribute page [3]. The exact phrase "colour is native to Indian seeing" does not appear in anything fetched; it is a paraphrase of his argument, not a quotation.
- Brand-side commercial photographers (Zomato, Swiggy, CRED, Nykaa, Tata, Jio, Myntra): nothing solid found. Searches returned listing-shoot vendors and campaign-marketing coverage, no named still photographer speaking about craft. The closest sourced commercial voice is Ashish Shah (Raw Mango, Byredo, Alexander McQueen) [21][22][23], plus a critic's teardown of the homogenised Indian fashion-campaign look [28].
- Surface vocabulary (distemper, lime wash, oxide floors, painted trucks, plastic buckets): the architecture and paint pages that name these finishes blocked fetching; only a designers' round-up on Indian colour [30] was retrievable. Treat those surface terms as INFERENCE unless a photographer names them below.

## What the medium actually is

**1. The founding argument is metaphysical, not decorative.** Raghubir Singh, writing in River of Colour (1998), set colour against the Western monochrome tradition on cultural grounds: "The fundamental condition of the West is one of guilt, linked to death, from which black is inseparable" and "The fundamental condition of India, however, is the cycle of rebirth, in which color is not just an essential element but also a deep inner source" [1][3]. A tribute essay quotes a further line from the same essay, "The true Indian artist cannot ignore the blessing of colour" [4]. The Met's own framing (reproduced verbatim by a third party [2]): Singh "embraced color as part of a continuous Indian aesthetic tradition that reaches back to the miniature paintings of the Mughal period (1526–1857)" and described his position as "on the Ganges side of modernism, rather than the Seine or East River side of it" [2]. Cartier-Bresson, his early mentor, rejected the colour work because for that generation "the real turned art only in shades of gray" [9].

**2. What Singh actually did with the frame (critics agree on this more than on anything else).**
- Density: the Met text calls the pictures "complex frieze-like compositions, teeming with incident, fractured by reflections, and pulsating with opulent color" [2]. Time Out: in the 1980s "he began to introduce multiple focal points", and by the mid-1990s the pictures "feature lush color, fractured space and a multiplicity of narratives, all within a single image" [5].
- The miniature-painting model: his style was "influenced by Mughal painting and Rajasthani miniature paintings, whose individual sections maintain their autonomy within the overall frame" [6]. Max Kozloff: "If you can imagine what a Rajput miniaturist could have learned from Henri Cartier-Bresson, you'll have a glimmer of Raghubir Singh's aesthetic" [6].
- Colour as the flattening agent: Museum Ludwig's Kolkata text says he used colour "to direct the viewer's attention across the entire picture so that the foreground and background often appear as if on a single plane" [8]; the same text describes "a partition wall and a raised floor create a construction of a picture in a picture" and cropping that presents "everyday situations in public places as distilled events, such as an argument in traffic or trading in front of the stock exchange" [8].
- Reflections and glass: the Bombay pictures look "through the glass walls of a jewellery shop, as if through a looking-glass, at the hustle and bustle of the market" [10]. Singh told V.S. Naipaul that glass and mirrors provide "an element of ambiguity and the sense of movement"; Naipaul on the result: "You cannot take it all in at once. You have to let your eye move from centre to centre, so there is constant movement in the picture" [11]. Pavement Mirror Shop, Howrah (1991) is the canonical example: "passersby reflected and refracted in the store's wares" [5].
- The still anchor inside the swirl: curator Mia Fineman, "Hands, gestures, and gazes swirl around a still center point, usually anchored by an individual who pointedly ignores the camera or stares back at it with confrontational directness" [11]. Fineman also recalled that on the street "you could see his eyes marking out the four corners of frames-to-be" [7].
- Frame within frame and foreground obstruction: Ratik Asokan describes the Subhas Chandra Bose statue picture as "framed by the pane-less square window of an olive-green open door crookedly hanging in the foreground, which in turn is cut off by an anonymous human hand" [12]; Santasil Mallik reads the same device as the statue's "(historical) framing ... literalised by the window frame of the truck's door" and the Benares rickshaw picture as "a medley of de-centred human actions, animals, objects, and symbols" with "disparate directions of attention" [13].
- The car door series: in A Way into India the Ambassador "becomes a camera obscura. Singh uses its doors and windshield to frame and divide his photographs" [6]; John Baldessari compared the fragmentation of space to Mondrian [3]. The Smithsonian exhibition text quotes the book's own claim to "every shade of color from the peacock's plume to the black of the elephant to the weathered landscape of the farmer's face" [14].
- Colour chords, as named by critics: "the vermillion of women's saris, the saffron of sadhus' robes, the ruby of turbans" mingled with "the kaleidoscopic, eye-popping colors of Westernization" [9]; NPR's picture captions name red wedding parties, fuchsia baskets, terracotta landscapes, green monsoon skies, a fruit seller's yellow and red, rangolis at doorways, painted walls [1]; a red Ambassador that "glows" against "the brown desert world" in early-morning light [4]; a "maroon dhoti ... which matches the colour of the shrine beside him" and "two garments, yellow and violet, hang to dry" [12]. Asokan's critique is worth keeping too: he argues Singh "imagined his countrymen as a backdrop for cultural ideas" [12], so colour-matching can tip into arranging people as decor.

**3. The black-and-white street generation (Rai, Bartholomew, Sheth, Taraporevala) teaches gesture and tilt, not colour.** Their shared street work was monochrome [15]; the lesson for a colour prompt is how they hold a crowd. Raghu Rai's stated reservation about colour is the useful counter-voice: "Colour, he believed, could draw the eye too quickly, offering an immediate satisfaction that risked bypassing deeper engagement", his pictures building force through "A glance, a gesture, a relationship between figures within a frame" [16]. On light he is blunt: "we get up early in the morning for good light, before the sun rises ... When the sun goes up, and light becomes harsh ... that is when I cannot shoot" [17]. His colour book is blurbed as the India of "the simple people ... their dignity and sense of colour and self-adornment as well as the earthy beauty of their humble homes" [18]. Ketaki Sheth's street frames "extend beyond the edges, they are often tilted and they bloom, making her vision of the street an angular experience"; Rai's boy "laughing as he flees" is "a spillage of time"; Taraporevala brings "cinematic stillness" [15]. Sheth on method: "when you are a fly on the wall ... you seize the moment" [19]. Pablo Bartholomew is described by ICP as combining documentation "with a penchant for unsettling, Surrealist-inspired juxtapositions" in "his attempt to portray an India free of picturesque clichés" [20].

**4. The contemporary art generation defines itself against the saturated image.** Each of these photographers arrived at colour (or refused it) as a reaction to the exotic postcard:
- Gauri Gill chose black and white for Rajasthan because "I wished to turn away from the brightly coloured photographs of Rajasthan made by neo colonial photographers who were focussing on what they saw as an 'exotic' place", preferring "the emotional register ... as well as the shimmering mirage like heat and light of the desert" [24]. Her later colour series Acts of Appearance is "vibrant color" but built on "everyday village activities" [25]; her rule of thumb: "the universal is often achieved by looking at the local or the quotidian, things that are familiar and therefore not exotic, but never ordinary" [25].
- Bharat Sikka: "I broke the connection of colour, and that's where The Sapper comes from"; "I didn't want to go through the clichés; everything was polluted everything was grey, it was sucking the colour, it was chaotic, very dark"; "I didn't want to introduce those exotic elements about India"; the frames should be "a little bit starker, cleaner, more constructed, more lines with perspectives", with Edward Hopper as "the painterly quality and the solitude, the light" [26]. UNVERIFIED (search snippet of an unfetched 1854 article): The Sapper is "cast in natural, neutral tones, often lit by a strong sun".
- Vasantha Yogananthan works "a very short period just before sunrise, thirty to forty-five minutes, when the light is already here, but not yet bright"; fog puts the landscape "in another zone"; "I have strived not to make the project exotic. No-one is wearing costumes or carrying props and there are almost no women wearing saris, it's not the Steve McCurry India"; composition is "symmetry ... converging around a central point of focus" or "an alignment of poles, buildings or trees", and "I definitely look more at cinema and painting than at photography" [27].
- Dayanita Singh found colour by accident: shooting factories "just after sunset" without black-and-white film, "She reached, instead, for a roll of colour daylight film, even though the light at the time was inappropriate for its use", producing a melancholic blue in which "Singh had finally found her own way with colour" [31]; Blue Book was her attempt to "learn to speak in colour" [32]; Dream Villa is night "illuminated and electrified by artificial light and the moon is just ornamentation" [33].
- Sohrab Hura pushes the other way: the Huis Marseille text notes "The soft colours of summer and winter, and images with implicit meanings, are replaced by bright colours, confrontational close-ups" and "hyper-realistic chaos" [34]; a reviewer describes The Coast as "hypercolor digital and hot flash", a palette "red/orange and lurid" [35]. His stated reason to photograph "with love" is that "We're always victims of the western media that displays these parts of the world and the people in them as passively enduring suffering" [36].

**5. The commercial strand.** Ashish Shah, who shoots for Raw Mango, Byredo and Alexander McQueen [22], states the goal directly: "By moving away from the stereotypical highly saturated and colorful documentary images of India that focus on the two extremes of exoticness and poverty, I'm hoping to give a truer representation of everything contemporary India offers" [21]. Aperture credits him with "the repertoire of attitudes and gestures through which the Indian body reposes, coupled with his attention to local complexions, styles, and settings" and notes "His depictions of backbreaking labor are unfailingly relieved by moments of languor"; his own diagnosis: "Often fashion imagery in India is very alienated from the world it represents" [22]. His Raw Mango wedding campaign is described through domestic detail: white sheets on beds, plain black-framed family portraits, "long uncarpeted corridors" [23]. The counter-critique (Shriya Zamindar) is that this look has itself congealed into a formula, the "genda phool effect" [28] (see cliches).

**Synthesis (INFERENCE from the above):** the craft consensus is that Indian colour is a matter of placement and time of day, not saturation. Singh's colour is structural (it flattens, matches, and rhymes across planes); the contemporaries either leave colour, mute it, shoot before the sun is up, or deliberately overdrive it with flash. Nobody credible describes their target as "vibrant". "Vibrant" and "highly saturated" appear in these sources only as the thing being rejected [21][24].

## What separates a convincing result from a generic one

1. **Colour is placed, not poured.** Singh's critics do not praise saturation; they praise structure: colour that flattens near and far into one plane [8], colour that rhymes across the frame (dhoti to shrine, garment to garment) [12], one glowing accent in a muted field [4]. A generic image maximises chroma everywhere; a convincing one has a chord.
2. **The frame is crowded but anchored.** "Teeming with incident" [2] plus a "still center point" [11]. Generic stock either isolates one smiling subject with shallow depth of field or gives an undifferentiated crowd. The craft is several autonomous sections [6] and one person who refuses the camera or stares it down [11].
3. **Something in the near foreground cuts the picture.** A door edge, a hand, a windshield, a shopfront's glass [10][12][13]. This is the single most repeated compositional observation across the Singh literature and is absent from stock.
4. **Time of day is specific and early.** Pre-sunrise for Yogananthan [27], before the sun rises for Rai, with harsh midday explicitly refused [17]. Generic "India" imagery is shot in bright sun and then warmed in post; INFERENCE: this is where the "golden light" cliche comes from [29].
5. **The traditional and the modern collide inside one frame.** Singh's vermillion and saffron "mingled with the kaleidoscopic, eye-popping colors of Westernization" [9]; a bicycle being washed while an elephant crosses a bus terminal [9]; wealth and precarity "often separated by little more than a street or a frame" [10]. Stock removes the modern to keep the timeless; the craft keeps both.
6. **People are doing ordinary things.** Gill's masks are worn during "everyday village activities" [25]; Hura's Kashmir includes "making a snowman or playing cricket" [36]; Shah's bodies "repose" and labour is "relieved by moments of languor" [22]. Bartholomew's whole project is described as an India "free of picturesque clichés" [20].
7. **The domestic interior is plain, not dressed.** White sheets, plain black-framed portraits, uncarpeted corridors [23]; "humble homes" whose beauty is "earthy" [18]; a photo studio's actual red drapes and ageing lights [37][38]. Generic imagery adds brass, marigold garlands and carved wood to signal India.
8. **Restraint is a legitimate outcome.** Three of the most respected contemporary photographers reduced or abandoned colour to escape the postcard (Gill [24], Sikka [26], and Dayanita Singh's blue-cast accident [31]). A prompt that demands "vibrant" is asking for the thing the practitioners left.

## The cliches to avoid (with who named them)

- **Snake charmers.** Named by a curator (surname Joshi) quoted in NPR's coverage: Singh "showed to foreigners, or rather everyone, that India is more than a land of snake-charmers" [1]; and by Chris Wiley describing Aaryan Sinha's early work: "snake charmers, corpse immolation on the Ganges, and pilgrim handprints on temple walls" [29].
- **"Snakes, steaming trains and moustachioed men."** Homegrown's critique of the McCurry tradition, described as a "Slumdog Millionaire-depiction" made for Western consumption; the same piece quotes Debmalya Ray Choudhuri on the technical tell, "the use of flash to separate people into layers" that treats subjects "like dummies and puppets" [40].
- **"Stereotypical scenes that wide-eyed Westerners might snap on vacation."** Magnum's Rafal Milach to Aaryan Sinha [29].
- **"It's not the Steve McCurry India."** Vasantha Yogananthan: no costumes, no props, almost no women in saris [27].
- **"The brightly coloured photographs of Rajasthan made by neo colonial photographers."** Gauri Gill [24].
- **"Highly saturated and colorful documentary images of India that focus on the two extremes of exoticness and poverty."** Ashish Shah [21].
- **"A place of profound poverty and magical mysticism, brilliant colour and heartbreaking pain."** Huck, introducing Kapil Das's counter-work on "the peripatetic spaces of our lives that we see but often do not notice" [41].
- **"Material-poverty-and-spiritual-richness" imagery, especially from Varanasi; NGO subjects "eternally smiling" or "eternally sad".** PhotoMail [42].
- **The Instagram-India list.** Deepti Dadlani: fishing nets, train windows, craftspeople bent over looms, women in bandhani carrying water pots, trains cutting through countryside, "narrow bazaars in golden light", weathered doorways, village homes, artisan hands at work; "The aesthetic stays in focus; everything else fades into atmosphere" [39].
- **The fashion-campaign formula, the "genda phool effect".** Shriya Zamindar: "bright marigolds in a phool mandi, Varanasi, Malyali pedestrians in mundus", grain-heavy high-contrast film, every collection set in a cottage with family portraits, women styled "to fry jalebis" [28]. This is the cliche that most directly threatens a "candid Indian lifestyle" brief, because it is the current tasteful default, not the tourist one.
- **The grey opposite.** Bharat Sikka names the inverse cliche too: "everything was polluted everything was grey, it was sucking the colour, it was chaotic, very dark" [26]. Avoiding the postcard by going grim is also a template.
- **"Passively enduring suffering."** Sohrab Hura on Western media's Kashmir [36].
- Of the brief's own list (cows, turbans, Holi powder, temple bells, Taj Mahal): no fetched critic names cows, temple bells or the Taj explicitly. Turbans appear only inside Singh's palette ("the ruby of turbans" [9]), as a colour, not as a cliche. Holi appears only as a designer's context for rani pink [30]. Nothing solid found naming these five as cliches; treat their exclusion as INFERENCE from the pattern above (props, costume, timelessness, festival colour).

## Proposed clause phrasings (concrete, each with rationale + source #)

Each clause is written to drop into a prompt as-is. Positive clauses first, then the exclusion clause.

1. **"Several things happening at once across a wide frame, each part legible on its own, no single hero."**
   Rationale: the Singh composition as the Met and Time Out describe it ("teeming with incident", "multiple focal points") and the miniature-painting logic of autonomous sections. [2][5][6]

2. **"One person near the centre holds still and either ignores the camera or looks straight into it; hands, gestures and gazes move around them."**
   Rationale: Fineman's description of the Singh anchor; gives the model an anchor and a crowd behaviour in one sentence. [11]

3. **"Something in the near foreground cuts the picture: the edge of an open door, a car's door frame and windshield, a shopfront's glass, a hand."**
   Rationale: the frame-within-frame and foreground-obstruction devices named by Asokan, Mallik and the Ambassador series. [6][12][13]

4. **"Part of the scene is seen as a reflection in glass or a hanging mirror, so near and far overlap."**
   Rationale: "fractured by reflections", Pavement Mirror Shop, the Bombay jewellery shop looking-glass, Naipaul on the eye moving centre to centre. [2][5][10][11]

5. **"Colour carries the eye across the whole picture so background and foreground sit on one plane; keep everything in focus."**
   Rationale: Museum Ludwig's account of how Singh's colour flattens depth; a direct instruction against shallow depth of field. [8]

6. **"One or two colour rhymes across the frame (a maroon cloth answering a maroon wall; a yellow and a violet garment on a line), not colour everywhere."**
   Rationale: Asokan's observed colour-matching in Singh; makes "colourful" into a structure. [12]

7. **"Traditional pigments beside modern plastic and paint: vermillion or saffron cloth next to a fuchsia basket, painted signage, a terracotta wall, a green monsoon sky."**
   Rationale: the Singh chord as Musee Magazine and NPR describe it; the collision of old pigment and new plastic is the subject, not the exoticism. [1][9]

8. **"Palette named by pigment: haldi yellow, marigold, rust, rani pink, geru red ochre, indigo, Jodhpur blue."**
   Rationale: the terms Indian designers use for the national palette; pigment nouns are steadier than adjectives. Surface terms such as distemper, lime wash and oxide floor are UNVERIFIED from photographic sources; use them only if the brief accepts INFERENCE. [30]

9. **"Shot in the half hour before sunrise, light present but not yet bright, a little haze in the far planes; never harsh overhead sun."**
   Rationale: Yogananthan's stated working window and Rai's refusal of harsh sun; a precise window that also steers away from the "golden light" bazaar cliche. [17][27][39]

10. **"For a desert or hot-season exterior: shimmering, mirage-like heat and light, colours a step muted rather than saturated."**
    Rationale: Gill's description of Rajasthan and her explicit rejection of the saturated neo-colonial picture. [24]

11. **"For night: the scene lit by its own artificial light, the sky and moon as ornament, with the cool blue of daylight-balanced film after sunset."**
    Rationale: Dayanita Singh's Dream Villa and Blue Book, the only sourced account of an Indian photographer's night colour. [31][33]

12. **"For a quiet domestic frame: starker, cleaner, constructed, straight lines and perspective, neutral tones, the solitude of a Hopper interior."**
    Rationale: Sikka's own words for The Sapper; the restrained pole. The "strong sun" detail is UNVERIFIED (search snippet only). [26]

13. **"An Indian home as it is: white cotton bedsheets, plain black-framed family portraits, a long uncarpeted corridor, the earthy beauty of a humble house; no added garlands or brass."**
    Rationale: the Raw Mango campaign's domestic detail plus Rai's "earthy beauty of their humble homes"; the exclusion counters the genda phool formula. [18][23][28]

14. **"Bodies at rest in the way Indian bodies actually rest: squatting, leaning, one foot tucked; labour relieved by languor; local complexions kept true."**
    Rationale: Aperture on Ashish Shah's repertoire of attitudes and gestures, and his attention to local complexions. The specific postures are INFERENCE; the principle is sourced. [22]

15. **"A candid moment made of a glance, a gesture, a relationship between two figures; fly on the wall, nobody performing for the camera."**
    Rationale: Rai's unit of meaning and Sheth's method; both named the candid the same way. [16][19]

16. **"For a street frame: the camera a little tilted, the composition running past the edges, a figure mid-motion (skipping a puddle, laughing as he runs)."**
    Rationale: Mansukhani on Sheth and Rai in The Passerby; the only sourced description of how the Indian street generation actually cropped. [15]

17. **"Ordinary activity, not spectacle: a familiar thing that is never ordinary."**
    Rationale: Gill's test, restated as an instruction. [25]

18. **Exclusion clause: "No snake charmers, sadhus, costumes or props; no marigold flower market, no bazaar washed in golden light, no weathered doorway, no woman with a water pot; no grain-heavy high-contrast film look; no saturated poverty-or-exotic extremes; and no grey polluted chaos as the alternative."**
    Rationale: assembled from what critics and practitioners named explicitly (Yogananthan, Sinha and Milach, Dadlani, Zamindar, Shah, Sikka). Cows, temple bells, Holi powder and the Taj are not named by any fetched source; add them only as INFERENCE. [21][26][27][28][29][39]

19. **Only when the flash register is wanted: "Direct on-camera flash at night, hypercolour, red-orange and lurid, confrontational close-ups."**
    Rationale: Hura's The Coast as described by Huis Marseille and a reviewer; this is a distinct look, not a default, and the Homegrown critique warns that flash-separated layers read as "dummies and puppets" when misused. [34][35][40]
